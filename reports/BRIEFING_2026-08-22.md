# Morning Briefing — August 22, 2026

## This Week's Themes
**Psychological safety is under siege from multiple directions.** Three stories this week—anger as a leadership tool, Meta's CTO publicly humiliating an employee, and perfection cultures killing innovation—all point to the same thing: leaders are actively destroying the conditions that make organizations function well, and some are doing it proudly. **Incentive misalignment is the structural root of innovation failure.** Two Inc pieces and the MIT Sloan article converge on the same insight: organizations say they want innovation but have built systems that punish it—through incentives, workload overload, and performance management that rewards flawless execution over intelligent risk. **AI is creating new accountability and equity problems faster than organizations can solve them.** The BBC piece on AI recruitment bias and the HBR piece on employees masking AI-generated decisions reveal two sides of the same coin: AI is being deployed into organizational systems without adequate governance, creating both fairness violations and perverse behavioral adaptations. **Gender equity in organizations remains a systems design problem, not an individual resilience problem.** The BBC and Fast Company pieces both show how organizational structures—algorithmic screening criteria, performance evaluation systems—systematically disadvantage women while framing the burden of adaptation as theirs to bear.

## Top Story
**"When Employees Are Held Accountable for AI-Generated Decisions" (HBR)** — This is the story Pete's audience isn't ready for but needs to hear. Every CHRO and COO is deploying AI tools right now, but almost none have redesigned accountability structures to match. The finding that employees are covertly reshaping and masking AI outputs to protect their credibility means organizations are getting neither the value of the AI nor the transparency of human judgment—they're getting a performance of both. This is an urgent operating system design problem, not a training problem.

Pete could unpack the research on non-promotable tasks (Linda Babcock's work), connect it to construct validity failures in performance appraisal, and offer a concrete diagnostic framework CHROs can use to audit where invisible relational labor is being performed and by whom. It's the kind of piece that lets Pete demonstrate real expertise—moving from "this is unfair" to "here's exactly how your performance management system is encoding this bias and here are four structural interventions." The four-fix framework in the article gives natural scaffolding for a longer analysis.
