# The 8 habits of great conversationalists

**Source:** Fast Company  
**Published:** 2026-09-15T10:00:00  
**URL:** [https://www.fastcompany.com/91604120/the-8-habits-of-great-conversationalists](https://www.fastcompany.com/91604120/the-8-habits-of-great-conversationalists)

## Summary
Based on hundreds of lunches with strangers, the author identifies 8 habits of great conversationalists: (1) being curious rather than performing, (2) listening to understand rather than to reply, (3) being comfortable with pauses, (4) not trying to 'win' conversations, (5) matching energy rather than imposing it, (6) asking simple questions, (7) staying present even when the topic isn't exciting, and (8) making people feel safe. The core insight is that conversational skill is about learnable behaviors—attention, patience, and responsiveness—not innate personality traits.

## Organizational Psychology Angle
These habits map directly onto psychological safety, active listening, and relational coordination—foundational constructs in team effectiveness and leadership communication. The emphasis on creating safety, matching energy, and collaborative (not competitive) dialogue mirrors research on high-performing teams and trust-building in organizations.

## Leadership Lesson
Leaders and HR executives should treat conversational quality as a trainable competency, not a personality trait. Embedding these habits—especially deep listening, comfort with silence, and creating psychological safety—into leadership development programs and coaching can improve 1:1s, feedback conversations, and cross-functional collaboration.

## Original Story Summary
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">When people talk about being &#8220;good at conversation,&#8221; they often imagine something innate. They think of charisma, wit, or the ability to hold court effortlessly. But after hundreds of lunches with strangers, I came to see conversation very differently. The best conversationalists I met weren’t necessarily the loudest, funniest, or most assertive people in the room. In fact, many of them were quiet, understated, and unassuming.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">What set them apart wasn’t personality, but habit.&nbsp;Strong communicators behaved consistently, from how they approached conversations to how they treated people. And because their behaviors were repeatable, they could be learned.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Here are some of the habits I have found to be common among those who are great conversationalists:</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-habit-1-they-re-curious-not-performing">Habit 1: They’re curious, not performing</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Great conversationalists aren’t trying to impress. They’re trying to understand. This distinction matters. When someone enters a conversation focused on how they’re coming across, their attention is split. Part of them is listening while another is monitoring themselves. That self-consciousness gets in the way.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The people who stood out were those who seemed genuinely interested in the person across from them. They asked questions not to fill silence, but to satisfy curiosity. &nbsp;They followed threads naturally rather than steering the conversation towards familiar ground.&nbsp;When you’re genuinely interested in someone else, you stop monitoring yourself so closely. And when that self-consciousness fades, the other person feels less scrutinized too. The conversation stops feeling like a performance and becomes a friendly exchange.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-habit-2-they-listen-to-understand-not-to-reply">Habit 2: They listen to understand, not to reply</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This sounds obvious, but it’s surprisingly rare. Many people listen just long enough to prepare their next comment. They wait for gaps and scan for openings. As a result, their responses are only loosely connected to what someone said before.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Strong conversationalists do the opposite. They listen with the intention of understanding. They build their responses directly on what they’ve heard. They ask follow-up questions that show they’ve been paying attention. I’ve found that this habit helped conversations flow. When someone felt understood, they relaxed, elaborated, and shared more freely. They deepened the conversations without effort.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-habit-3-they-re-comfortable-with-pauses">Habit 3: They’re comfortable with pauses</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Silence makes many people uneasy. They rush to fill it the moment they feel any sort of awkwardness or judgement. But the best conversationalists I met didn’t seem bothered by pauses at all. They let conversations breathe. A brief silence gives people space to think. It signals that reflection is welcome. It also removes the pressure to entertain. Some of the most interesting moments during the lunches emerged after a pause, when someone chose to say something more thoughtful than their first instinct. Being comfortable with silence is something you practice, not something you’re born with. It comes from trusting that conversation doesn’t need constant motion to be engaging.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-habit-4-they-don-t-try-to-win">Habit 4: They don’t try to ‘win’</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Another pattern I noticed was how rarely good conversationalists tried to prove a point. They weren’t trying to sound clever or knowledgeable. They didn’t dominate discussions or correct others unnecessarily. Even when they disagreed, they did so gently. This made conversations feel collaborative rather than competitive, and allowed us to explore ideas together. Trying to &#8220;win&#8221; a conversation usually backfires. It raises stakes, creates tension, and narrows what people are willing to share. Letting go of that impulse can open things up.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-habit-5-they-match-energy-rather-than-impose-it">Habit 5: They match energy rather than impose it</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Some people bring a fixed energy into every interaction. They’re always upbeat, always intense, or always reserved. Great conversationalists are more adaptive. They read the room. They adjust their pace and tone. They don’t force enthusiasm where it doesn’t fit, and they don’t withdraw when engagement is welcome. This doesn’t mean they lack authenticity. It means they’re responsive. They recognize that conversation is something you build together, not something one person controls. This habit made interactions feel natural rather than strained, because it allowed the conversation to settle into its natural rhythm.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-habit-6-they-ask-simple-questions">Habit 6: They ask simple questions</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">One of the most surprising lessons from the lunches was how effective simple questions can be.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Questions like, &#8220;What do you enjoy about that?&#8221; or &#8220;How did you end up there?&#8221; or even, &#8220;Do you have any kids?&#8221; consistently led to rich conversation. They didn’t require clever phrasing or insider knowledge. They required interest. People often underestimate simple questions because they don’t want to sound unsophisticated. In reality, simplicity lowers barriers. It invites people to explain things in their own words and share what matters to them. Complex questions can impress. Simple questions connect.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-habit-7-they-stay-present-even-when-the-topic-isn-t-exciting">Habit 7: They stay present even when the topic isn’t exciting</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Not every conversation is fascinating. Not every topic resonates. The best conversationalists didn’t check out when the subject drifted away from their interests. They stayed engaged. This doesn’t mean feigning enthusiasm. It means respecting the other person’s perspective. When people feel like others merely tolerate them, they retreat. But when they feel acceptance, they open up. Being present is a habit. It’s the choice to give someone your attention, even when the payoff isn’t immediate.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-habit-8-they-make-people-feel-safe">Habit 8: They make people feel safe</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Perhaps the most important habit of all is the ability to make others feel safe. Safe to speak. Safe to disagree. Safe to be unsure. One of my lunches was with a very likeable lawyer in a harborside cafe. She made me feel at ease up front by making a self-deprecating joke about her surname.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Throughout the lunch, she was warm and unhurried. When I hesitated midsentence, she didn’t jump in. She waited. Simple things that made it feel like we were on the same team. She told me she explained complex matters to her clients the same way—in plain English rather than legalese, often starting with, &#8220;Here’s what this actually means.&#8221; People trusted her quickly, and they opened up.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-not-judged-or-rushed">Not judged or rushed</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The lunches taught me that people share more when they sense that the other person won’t judge or rush them. Safety doesn’t come from saying the right things; it comes from how you respond. Great conversationalists create an atmosphere where people don’t feel the need to impress. And when that pressure disappears, authenticity takes its place.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">What all of these habits have in common is that they’re about <em>actions</em>, not personality. None require extroversion, confidence, or quick thinking. They require attention, patience, and practice. I met excellent conversationalists from across the personality spectrum. Some were outgoing. Others were reserved. What united them was not who they were, but how they behaved. That’s good news. Habits can be learned.</p>
