# Leadership now means thinking across industries

**Source:** Fast Company  
**Published:** 2026-08-12T19:08:00  
**URL:** [https://www.fastcompany.com/91588060/leadership-now-means-thinking-across-industries](https://www.fastcompany.com/91588060/leadership-now-means-thinking-across-industries)

## Summary
The article argues that modern leadership requires cross-industry thinking—the ability to recognize patterns, coordination challenges, and value-creation strategies across disparate sectors (e.g., superyacht building, universities, healthcare). It advocates for leadership education that teaches leaders to translate (not copy) practices between industries, holding ethics and context at the center.

## Organizational Psychology Angle
The piece highlights boundary-spanning leadership as a core competency, connecting to research on analogical reasoning, cognitive flexibility, and the transfer of organizational practices across domains. It implicitly argues that silo-based expertise creates cognitive rigidity that limits institutional adaptation.

## Leadership Lesson
CHROs and COOs should deliberately expose leaders to unfamiliar industries and cross-sector case studies—not to import practices wholesale, but to develop pattern recognition and adaptive translation skills that improve organizational design, stakeholder management, and innovation.

## Strength Score
**6/10** — The article makes a genuine organizational psychology argument about boundary-spanning leadership, cognitive flexibility, and cross-domain knowledge transfer. However, it is largely conceptual and opinion-driven, with a single illustrative example (Tankoa Yachts) rather than empirical evidence or deep organizational analysis. The org psych connection is real but the treatment is more thought-leadership than applied research.

## Original Story Summary
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">When we picture leaders, we still tend to imagine experts anchored firmly in one sector—finance, education, health, or technology—each fluent in its own metrics and jargon. We rarely imagine leaders whose core skill is crossing boundaries: learning from a superyacht builder how to rethink a university or borrowing ideas from luxury crafts to redesign public institutions.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Yet that is precisely the kind of leadership our world now demands.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Modern organizations operate in ecosystems, not silos. A university depends on technology platforms and hospitality standards. A hospital borrows logistics thinking from retail. Cultural institutions quietly use data models pioneered in finance. The challenges these organizations face—trust, sustainability, inclusion, long‑term value—do not respect sector boundaries. They stretch across industries, jurisdictions, and disciplines.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Leadership education, however, hasn&#8217;t caught up to this interconnected reality. We still train leaders to climb narrow ladders: master your industry’s tools, internalize its benchmarks, become the best in your niche. What we rarely teach is how to move calmly and intelligently across industries, to recognize the patterns in one domain, and adapt them responsibly in another. Cross‑industry leadership is the practice of doing exactly that.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-what-a-shipyard-can-teach-leaders"><strong>What a shipyard can teach leaders</strong><strong></strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Consider Tankoa Yachts, a Genoa‑based shipyard specializing in custom superyachts. On paper, it sits in a niche: luxury, craft, a very particular kind of client. In reality, Tankoa’s work touches design, engineering, global supply chains, environmental regulation, <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/branding" title="Branding">branding</a>, and long‑term client relationships all at once. Building a yacht is not just building a vessel. It is orchestrating dozens of disciplines into one coherent experience over many years, under high expectations.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Leaders at Tankoa cannot think only as shipbuilders. They must think beyond the yard—about how a yacht will be used, what it will signal, and how its footprint interacts with evolving norms of sustainability and responsibility in the luxury sector. They balance tradition and innovation: Tankoa’s heritage of Italian design on one side and new materials, propulsion technologies, and environmental standards on the other. Their work is a continuous negotiation between craft, technology, and values.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Why should a rector, a public official, or a social entrepreneur care about a yacht yard? Because these three underlying leadership questions are strikingly familiar:</p>



<ol class="wp-block-list">
<li>How do you create something highly customized without losing coherence? </li>



<li>How do you invest in long‑term relationships rather than short‑term transactions? </li>



<li>How do you honor craft and expertise while still opening doors to innovation?</li>
</ol>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Tankoa answers those questions in steel and teak. Universities and public institutions answer them in curricula, services, and policies. Cross‑industry leadership begins by recognizing that connection.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-translating-craft-into-curriculum"><strong>Translating craft into curriculum</strong><strong></strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Higher education often imagines itself as the opposite of luxury manufacturing. It is public‑minded, mission‑driven, and constantly negotiating resource constraints. Yet it faces some of the same systemic questions that a shipyard does.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Like Tankoa, a university designs complex experiences for a relatively small number of people at a time—students, researchers, and staff across different programs. It must align architecture, technology, pedagogy, and community life into something coherent and meaningful. It carries a legacy: traditions, disciplines, expectations. And it faces a world of shifting regulations, environmental pressures, and social demands.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Cross‑industry leadership asks: What can universities learn from how Tankoa manages client expectations over long projects? How might academic leaders borrow the logic of bespoke craft to redesign student journeys—without importing the exclusivity of luxury? How can institutions emulate the way a shipyard coordinates multiple expert teams while still giving each space to take pride in their work?</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The point is not to turn campuses into yacht clubs. It is to recognize that leadership, at its core, is about integrating diverse forms of expertise into one shared project—whether that project is a vessel crossing oceans or a public university serving a city.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-a-new-literacy-for-leaders"><strong>A new literacy for leaders</strong><strong></strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Tomorrow’s leaders will need a new kind of literacy. They will have to read across industries: to look at a shipyard, a sports club, a health system, or a cultural institution and see underlying patterns of coordination, trust, and value creation. They will have to translate, not copy. Good cross‑industry leadership resists the temptation of superficial analogies like run a university like a startup, and instead adapts practices in ways that respect context, power, and purpose. They will have to hold ethics at the center, especially when borrowing from luxury sectors, choosing carefully which lessons are compatible with public missions and which must be left behind.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Leadership education must follow. It cannot restrict itself to case studies within one sector or treat industry experience as a narrow ladder. Programs should put unfamiliar sectors in the room—bringing shipyards to rectors, universities to manufacturers, and public institutions to startup founders. They should ask participants to map how a practice from one industry would need to be transformed to fit their own institution—not simply imported wholesale. And they should treat cross‑industry literacy as a core competency, alongside finance, strategy, or negotiation.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We once expanded leadership education to include sustainability when we finally understood that environmental risk was not external to business. Now we need to expand it again—to teach leaders to move across industries, see beyond their own playbooks, and redesign institutions with a wider, more connected imagination.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In that world, visiting a yacht yard is no longer a curiosity. It is part of the curriculum.</p>
