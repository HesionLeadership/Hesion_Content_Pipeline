## This Week's Themes

Three themes dominate this week. First, AI's impact on skill-building is contested: **The economist behind the 'China shock' says AI is a paradoxical 'skill-disequalizer' for Gen Z workers** (Fortune), **What Top Performers Do Differently with AI—and Why They See the Biggest Benefits** (HBR), and **CEOs are delegating AI before understanding it** (Fast Company) all probe whether AI tools build or erode real competence. Second, trust and transparency failures keep surfacing: **'Never seen anything quite like it': How rural Pennsylvania's $165 million data center fight became representative of the bipartisan battle against AI** (Fortune), **An Anthropic AI model sent a false homicide tip to Philadelphia police** (TechCrunch), **Fired OpenAI safety researchers dispute misconduct claims, warn of chilling effect** (TechCrunch), and **Pentagon stops using Anthropic AI tools months after blacklisting company, BBC told** (BBC Business) all show gaps between stated policy and actual practice. Third, manager quality and talent mobility keep topping flashy perks or office mandates: **Ancestry's CEO says a billionaire boss taught him to eat lunch with employees in the break room—it gives him access to 'unfiltered information'** (Fortune), **Why a good boss matters more than working from home** (Fast Company), **Nvidia's secret to retaining staff? No org charts, a flat structure, and two extra days off every quarter** (Fortune), and **How Marriott's leadership pipeline is helping it navigate Europe's hospitality talent shortfall** (Fortune) reinforce that relationship quality and structured mobility drive retention more than location or credentials.

## Top Story

**The economist behind the 'China shock' says AI is a paradoxical 'skill-disequalizer' for Gen Z workers** (Fortune). This is the most consequential finding for Pete's audience: AI made junior lawyers look better in the moment but didn't make them better, while senior lawyers actually improved. That's a direct warning to CHROs about mistaking tool-assisted output for real development—and a case for redesigning apprenticeship pipelines before a generation of junior talent hits a skills cliff.

## Best Newsletter Candidate

**McKinsey research shows AI creating more jobs than automation displaces — but 11 million American workers will need help this decade** (Fortune). This one deserves the full 500+ words because it exposes a quiet contradiction at the heart of most talent strategies: organizations say they value skills but actually screen for credentials, and that gap—not a shortage of capable people—is what's blocking internal mobility. Pete can unpack the distinction between legally mandated credentials and mere hiring convention, show how that distinction silently shapes who gets considered for a promotion versus who gets passed over for an external hire, and connect it to the McKinsey finding that administrative assistants have the skills to become project managers but rarely get the chance. There's room to explore the psychological cost of this too—employees who sense their organization "doesn't practice what it preaches" on skills-based hiring will disengage from internal development efforts, creating a self-fulfilling prophecy where companies complain about talent shortages while sitting on underused internal talent. A newsletter here could walk CHROs through a practical audit: which of our screens are truly required by law or safety, versus just habit? Where have we hired externally for a role that someone internal could fill with the right stretch assignment? This is the kind of structural, systems-level diagnosis that differentiates Pete's thinking from generic "upskill your workforce" advice—it names the real barrier (credential conventions) rather than the symptom (skills gaps).

## Best Workshop/Keynote Example

**'Never seen anything quite like it': How rural Pennsylvania's $165 million data center fight became representative of the bipartisan battle against AI** (Fortune). This is a textbook, concrete illustration of procedural justice theory in action: a secretive, top-down rollout united Democrats and Republicans against it, and a governor had to reverse fast-track policy as a result. Great live example for a workshop on why perceived fairness in process often outweighs the substantive merits of a change initiative.
