# AI is making us sound the same—and killing our personal expression

**Source:** Fast Company  
**Published:** 2026-09-01T10:00:00  
**URL:** [https://www.fastcompany.com/91598016/ai-is-making-us-sound-the-same-and-killing-our-personal-expression](https://www.fastcompany.com/91598016/ai-is-making-us-sound-the-same-and-killing-our-personal-expression)

## Summary
Research published in Nature Human Behavior finds that AI-generated writing is homogenizing human expression, reducing stylistic diversity by 21-50% and eroding identity signals embedded in language. Over a third of web pages published since ChatGPT's launch were AI-authored, and the study warns this convergence could undermine cognitive diversity and collective problem-solving.

## Organizational Psychology Angle
The homogenization of written communication through AI tools threatens cognitive diversity within organizations—a critical ingredient for effective team problem-solving and innovation. When employees filter their writing through the same LLMs, organizations lose the identity signals and diverse perspectives that fuel creative friction and better decision-making.

## Leadership Lesson
CHROs and COOs should develop AI usage policies that encourage employees to draft original thinking before using AI for refinement, preserving the cognitive diversity that drives innovation. Leaders should also audit how AI tools may be flattening communication styles across their organizations, potentially masking valuable differences in perspective and reducing the quality of collective intelligence.

## Original Story Summary
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">More than a third of all internet web pages published since ChatGPT’s November 2022 launch were authored by <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/artificial-intelligence" title="AI">AI</a>, <a href="https://www.pewresearch.org/data-labs/2026/08/20/how-much-of-the-internet-is-written-with-ai/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">according to the Pew Research Center</a>. And the writing is becoming simpler than ever.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That flood of AI-generated language may also be changing the way humans write. A <a href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.11266" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">new paper</a> in <em>Nature Human Behavior</em> finds that LLMs can push people’s writing toward a narrower set of linguistic norms, reducing stylistic diversity and obscuring some of the identity signals embedded in our words.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Three years ago, lead researcher Zhivar Sourati read <em>The Secret Life of Pronouns: What Our Words Say About Us</em>, James Pennebaker’s  book that explored how people’s personal values, backgrounds, and beliefs can lead them to communicate the same ideas using different words. At the same time, LLMs were <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/90990376/what-we-can-learn-from-chatgpts-first-year" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">becoming more popular</a>, and Sourati began to wonder whether the tech might reduce that linguistic diversity.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;We were kind of asking, what would actually happen when everyone&#8217;s text is going through the same models?” Sourati says. “If you have this layer on top of everyone&#8217;s text, would you change any of these artifacts that we know about people&#8217;s texts? Would it affect it in any significant way? What are the implications?”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">So, he set to work to find out. First, Sourati conducted a time-series analysis using approximately 80,000 academic papers, 400,000 news articles, and 300,000 Reddit stories written before and after ChatGPT’s introduction. He found that after the adoption of AI writing tools, variation in writing complexity increasingly converged toward common stylistic norms. The trend was consistent across all three datasets.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Next, Sourati and his team conducted a more traditional experiment. They had GPT-3.5, Gemini, and Meta’s Llama 3 rewrite human-authored texts using a variety of prompts. While the models largely preserved the texts’ meaning, they reduced variation in writing complexity 21% to 50%.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">But Sourati wanted to get more specific about the societal implications: How are LLMs eroding the signals of individual identity in our writing?</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">His team turned to texts written by people whose personal traits had been identified through psychological questionnaires. As in the earlier experiment, they fed the texts through an LLM. Statistical models were then used to predict traits—including demographics, personality, empathy, and moral values—based on linguistic features. After the texts were polished with AI, predictive accuracy declined by an average of 6%.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In the study’s third stage, Sourati explored exactly what was being erased. The results weren’t uniform. LLMs altered different linguistic signals in different ways depending on the personal trait being analyzed.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Sourati believes relying on LLMs to do all of your writing could have dangerous implications. The kind of problem-solving necessary in today’s world, he argues, depends on cognitive diversity, not just linguistic diversity.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;That&#8217;s the scary part,&#8221; Sourati tells <em>Fast Company</em>. &#8220;Because if everyone is using LLMs, then they have the ability to rewrite what&#8217;s the socially correct way of saying something, or what&#8217;s a credible way of having a perspective.&#8221;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Sourati hopes there’s a future in which diversity becomes a design principle in the development of large language models. He believes the people most vulnerable to their homogenizing effects are those still developing their cognitive abilities, particularly students.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">He says people need to learn to think for themselves first. Then they can use LLMs more creatively.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;If you already know how to reason through something, then maybe you can use these models to kind of just have their good sides,&#8221; Sourati says, &#8220;without having all the destructive effects that they have on collective problem-solving abilities.&#8221;</p>
