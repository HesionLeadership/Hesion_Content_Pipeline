# Microsoft exec warned AI could create a ‘doom loop’ for the internet

**Source:** Fast Company  
**Published:** 2026-09-18T20:45:00  
**URL:** [https://www.fastcompany.com/91609660/microsoft-exec-warned-ai-could-create-a-doom-loop-for-the-internet](https://www.fastcompany.com/91609660/microsoft-exec-warned-ai-could-create-a-doom-loop-for-the-internet)

## Summary
Unsealed court documents reveal Microsoft's director of applied science called OpenAI's training on copyrighted content 'the largest theft of labor in human history' and warned that AI creates a 'doom loop' by destroying its own supply chain. OpenAI's head of ChatGPT privately admitted publishers face an 'existential threat' from AI products that are 'largely substitutive' of source material.

## Organizational Psychology Angle
This case illustrates a stark disconnect between what leaders privately believe about the ethics and sustainability of their business practices and what they publicly defend—a form of organizational hypocrisy that can erode trust and psychological safety internally when employees see contradictory messaging.

## Leadership Lesson
Leaders should ensure alignment between internal beliefs and external messaging. When executives privately acknowledge that their business model causes harm but publicly defend it, they risk creating cognitive dissonance throughout the organization, damaging credibility with employees, partners, and the public. CHROs should establish ethical guardrails and channels for internal dissent before contradictions surface in litigation.

## Original Story Summary
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Newly revealed court files show that what <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/artificial-intelligence" title="AI">AI</a> companies say behind the scenes doesn’t always line up with what they tell the public.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In a legal brief <a href="https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.612697/gov.uscourts.nysd.612697.1587.1.pdf" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">unsealed</a> this week, an executive at Microsoft described OpenAI’s choice to train its models on news articles and other human-published content as the “largest theft of labor in human history.” </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The brief came to light through a <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2023/12/27/business/media/new-york-times-open-ai-microsoft-lawsuit.html" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">copyright lawsuit</a> <em>The New York Times</em> filed against Microsoft and OpenAI in 2023 over the tech companies’ practice of using the paper’s editorial content to train artificial intelligence.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Brent Hecht, Microsoft’s director of applied science, made the statement, according to the <a href="https://nytimes.com/2026/09/17/technology/microsoft-openai-publishing-industry.html" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">brief</a>, and also called the process of training AI models on copyrighted works “an astonishing theft of unprecedented proportions.” </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The brief also revealed that OpenAI’s head of ChatGPT admitted that publishers like <em>The New York Times</em> “face an existential threat” from AI products, which he described as “largely substitutive, period”—that is, designed to replace their source material. Hecht also described large language models as “a product that destroys its supply chain.”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In its lawsuit, <em>The New York Times</em> names Copilot and ChatGPT specifically, noting that the products were created by “copying and using <em>millions</em> of <em>The Times</em>’s copyrighted news articles, in-depth investigations, opinion pieces, reviews, how-to guides, and more.”&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The newspaper argues that by mass-ingesting its content without permission in order to train large language models designed to replace newsrooms, the pair of AI companies violated copyright law. In the suit, the publisher accuses Microsoft and Open AI of seeking a “free ride,” leveraging the paper’s own monetary investment into quality journalism to “build substitutive products without permission or payment.” </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">“While Defendants engaged in wide-scale copying from many sources, they gave <em>Times</em> content particular emphasis when building their LLMs—revealing a preference that recognizes the value of those works,” the paper’s legal team stated in its <a href="https://nytco-assets.nytimes.com/2023/12/NYT_Complaint_Dec2023.pdf" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">suit</a>. </p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-publishers-take-on-ai">Publishers take on AI</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In the years since <em>The New York Times</em> filed its suit, parallel lawsuits from the <em>New York Daily News</em> and the Center for Investigative Reporting have been <a href="https://www.npr.org/2025/01/14/nx-s1-5258952/new-york-times-openai-microsoft" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">combined</a> into one consolidated effort that aims to hold AI companies to account. Other plaintiffs of record in the mega lawsuit now include the Intercept, Ziff Davis, and local newspapers like the <em>Chicago Tribune</em> and the <em>Orlando Sentinel</em>. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Executives at Microsoft and OpenAI have maintained that the business of powering their lucrative AI systems with news stories falls under <a href="https://fairuse.stanford.edu/overview/fair-use/what-is-fair-use/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">fair use</a>, a legal framework that protects “transformative” copies of copyrighted works. The definition of that term leaves plenty of room for interpretation, a tension that lies at the <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91399713/anthropic-pay-15-billion-book-authors-settle-ai-copyright-suit" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">heart of the debate</a> over generative AI’s existential dependence on copyrighted art, writing, and other human-crafted original material. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In the new brief, <em>The Times</em> points to AI executives’ private statements as evidence that their products were designed from the jump as a substitute for the material they were trained on, an argument against the fair use defense. The internal statements come from documents surfaced during discovery and from statements made in depositions with company executives. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">“Resolution of these issues for Plaintiffs as a matter of law will streamline this case for trial and, by rejecting Defendants’ efforts to recast free riding as fair use, recognize that the future not just of journalism but of responsible AI too depends on preserving incentives for humans to produce the creative works on which a healthy society depends,” the brief states.</p>
