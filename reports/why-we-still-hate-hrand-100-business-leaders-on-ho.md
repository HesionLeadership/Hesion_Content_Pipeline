# Why we still hate HR—and 100 business leaders on how to fix it

**Source:** Fast Company  
**Published:** 2026-07-30T09:00:00  
**URL:** [https://www.fastcompany.com/91570540/why-we-still-hate-hr-100-business-leaders-advice-how-to-fix-it](https://www.fastcompany.com/91570540/why-we-still-hate-hr-100-business-leaders-advice-how-to-fix-it)

## Summary
A comprehensive investigation into why employees and leaders still distrust HR despite decades of technological investment and elevated C-suite status, featuring survey data showing majority negative perceptions and recommendations from 91 business leaders on how to transform the function from compliance-focused administration to strategic talent and culture enabler.

## Organizational Psychology Angle
The article exposes a fundamental role conflict at the heart of HR: serving as both employee advocate and management enforcer creates structural distrust that no amount of technology or title inflation can resolve. This dual-agency problem undermines organizational trust and psychological safety, with 86% of workers reporting fear of their HR department.

## Leadership Lesson
CHROs and COOs should structurally separate HR's compliance/risk management functions from its talent development and culture-building roles, tie HR metrics to business outcomes like retention of high performers and manager effectiveness rather than process compliance, and ensure HR leaders have genuine business fluency by rotating them through operational roles—because when HR is measured only on administrative efficiency, it will always default to rule-keeping over talent strategy.

They were planning a dinner party “and someone came running into my office and said, ‘Why are you inviting the head of HR? The whole vibe’s gonna go away.’ And I was like, what are we going to be doing so wrong that HR can’t be in the room?”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Although this anecdote could be read as being about one buzzkill, or even the entire HR discipline being a party nonstarter, it&#8217;s much bigger than that.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Employees and company leaders alike have very strong feelings about HR—and not much of it is positive. In a series of Fast Company LinkedIn polls, more than 1,500 respondents expressed negative views about HR.</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>51% say HR is not very responsive when they bring it a concern.</li>



<li>75% say HR is ineffective/nonfactor in helping them achieve professional goals.</li>



<li>56% say HR is adding very little (33%) or no significant (23%) value to the organization.</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">These results reinforce <a href="https://www.myperfectresume.com/career-center/careers/basics/hr-perceptions-report" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">a March 2024 survey</a> of nearly 1,000 workers that revealed 86% of them were afraid of their Human Resources department.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In August 2005, Fast Company published a cover story that blared “<a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/53319/why-we-hate-hr" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Why We Hate HR</a>.” Since then, the human resources discipline has seemingly been wholly transformed. Increasing investment in technology (currently estimated at more than $50 billion globally) has delivered cloud-based platforms that provide workers greater flexibility, more communication tools, and access to benefits and payroll information from anywhere on their mobile devices. It has created vast libraries of custom-made learning and development curricula. Applicant tracking systems have streamlined the talent acquisition process.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The HR tech boom has spawned tens of thousands of companies, with big winners such as Workday ($36 billion market cap) and Rippling ($17 billion valuation) becoming sector giants.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The function has also achieved rarified corporate stature, as new C-level titles such as chief human resources officer (CHRO) and chief people officer (CPO) <a href="https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/chro-among-fastest-growing-executive-roles" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">have proliferated</a>. If a top complaint two decades ago was that HR didn’t have a seat at the strategic table, increasingly it does.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Some HR leaders have even become business celebrities! Patty McCord, chief talent officer at Netflix, created a 120-plus page “culture deck” that promoted “the keeper test” and other strategies to increase “talent density” for the overall good of the team. Sheryl Sandberg, then Facebook’s chief operating officer, described it as &#8220;the most important document ever to come out of Silicon Valley.&#8221; Google&#8217;s senior vice president of people operations, the ex-McKinsey consultant Laszlo Bock, turned his advocacy of &#8220;high-freedom&#8221; companies (those that granted autonomy to employees) into a best-selling book. And Google’s focus on people analytics further fueled the proliferation of HR tech.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">These days, it&#8217;s hard to imagine a book entitled <em>Work Rules!</em>, Bock&#8217;s bestseller, resonating with so many readers. Whatever progress HR made in the twenty-first century—via technology, greater responsibility, and worker-friendly policies such as expanded benefits and remote-work policies (facilitated by new tech tools and accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic)—has waned. It likely hasn&#8217;t helped, either, that HR is having to unwind flexible work policies for return-to-office mandates and serving as a key collaborator in rolling out <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/artificial-intelligence" title="AI">AI</a> tools that have workers fearing for their jobs.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The problem of why so many people still hate HR runs deep. Can HR be saved? In a word, yes, and the answer doesn’t lie in its wholesale elimination (<a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91370026/bolts-ceo-on-why-hes-axing-progressive-workplace-policies" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">sorry, Bolt CEO Ryan Breslow</a>) or trying to turn it into something it’s not (like Amazon’s people, experience, and technology team).</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We reached out to 100 business leaders—HR practitioners, recruiters, founders, and 66 CEOs—to develop some answers. They’re not only for HR pros but for all leaders thinking about how to create the companies they want.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In this Fast Company exclusive, you’ll learn:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>the key flashpoints in HR’s never-ending negotiation between workers and management</li>



<li>how HR practitioners are trying to improve the function internally</li>



<li>what leaders can do to go beyond mere cosmetics to fix the fundamental problems, and</li>



<li>ideas from 91 executives—including 66 CEOs—on how to fix HR.</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-it-s-called-talent-acquisition-until-you-get-hired">‘It’s called talent acquisition—until you get hired’</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Resentment toward HR has often already reached full pitch even before anyone gets a job offer. The <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91425252/recruiters-dish-on-ghost-jobs-why-companies-post-them-and-how-to-outsmart-them" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">sham job descriptions</a>. The black-hole career portals. The clueless recruiter reaching out about a post-anesthesia care unit nursing opportunity even though you&#8217;re a medtech product manager whose only brush with nursing was the broken wrist you suffered sophomore year in college.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Automation has transformed the talent acquisition part of HR from its former white-glove ideal into a Rube Goldberg contraption that seems designed primarily to drive job seekers bonkers. Monster’s <a href="https://www.monster.com/career-advice/research/application-black-box-report" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Application Black Box Report</a> for May does not paint a pretty picture of the current applicant tracking system (ATS) experience. What other conclusion can you draw from 54% of the 1,000 U.S. worker respondents supporting heavy regulation or an outright ban of ATS?</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Even those lucky enough to be actively pursued by a company’s internal recruiters may be in for a shock once they join the firm. “It’s called talent acquisition before you get hired,” says Janet Sherlock, a former Ralph Lauren executive who now heads the organizational design consultancy <a href="https://org.works/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Org.works</a>. “When you get hired, you become a ‘resource.’”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">As the writer Scott Raab once quipped: “The only thing worse than looking for work is finding it.” He made that remark long before the internet, bots, and AI turned job seeking into the soul-crushing hellscape it’s now become.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A huge part of the problem is that talent acquisition is the chunk of HR most ripe to get the AI treatment itself. “Business leaders are trying to figure out how to get the highest efficiency and <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/productivity" title="Productivity">productivity</a> at the lowest cost,” says Johnny Taylor, CEO of <a href="https://www.shrm.org/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">SHRM</a> (Society for Human Resource Management). Bots are cheaper than humans—at least initially, before factoring in the exorbitant costs of burnout, attrition, and replacement. (The employee training platform Techclass attaches <a href="https://www.techclass.com/resources/learning-and-development-articles/employee-experience-surveys-top-mistakes-hr-and-l-and-d-must-avoid-in-2026" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">a global price tag of $8.8 trillion</a> to attrition and lost productivity.)</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Which means, of course, that HR itself is exposed to the AI axe, especially entry-level roles or those focused on processing. SHRM’s State of AI in HR report classifies 9.3% of HR roles as “high risk” for AI replacement. “The recruiting and talent side, that’s who’s losing their jobs first,” says Shana Pederson, a longtime recruiter and a fellow at <a href="https://forhumanity.center/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">ForHumanity</a>, which independently audits AI systems to mitigate risk and build trust. “I’ve noticed [a disproportionate number] of out-of-work talent recruiters.”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Overreliance on bots to screen applicants may also create conflicts with state, federal, and international regulations. In Mobley v. Workday, a class-action lawsuit pending in federal court in California, plaintiffs allege that Workday’s AI-powered ATS resulted in discrimination by rejecting qualified candidates based on protected characteristics.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">SHRM’s Taylor sees obvious problems with AI screening tools but believes the technology will improve. “The recruitment process is by definition exclusionary,” he says. “We have one job and 500 people applied for it, so we’re excluding 499 people. If you believe that human beings can screen each one of those applications and give it a fair look, we just can’t. It’s not sustainable as there’s no business model to support it. So we need that HR technology.”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Taylor is confident that we can engineer bias out of machines more easily than we can remove it from humans, but For Humanity’s Pederson disagrees. “We can’t eliminate bias,” she says. “It is absolutely impossible, nor is it wise, to eliminate bias completely . . . . Part of what’s important about humans still remaining in the loop is that humans are the ones who are going to recognize where bias needs to be mitigated and why.”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Of the 66 CEOs who responded to our simple prompt—If you could recommend one meaningful change that would make any organization’s HR function more effective, what would it be?—40 shared recommendations that would reorient HR to refocus less on organizational processes and more on talent acquisition, development, and retention.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">“Give HR a P&amp;L stake in the workforce it supports,” says Shawn Galloway, CEO of the Houston-based workplace safety consultancy <a href="https://proactsafety.com/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">ProAct Safety</a>. “HR is still measured by process compliance and cost containment rather than by whether the business got better people, which insures it behaves like a rule keeper rather than a talent engine. Tie the function’s own scorecard to the performance of the people it hires, keeps, and develops, and the henchman-for-the-CFO reflex disappears on its own.”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">But many of the CEO suggestions are more directional, if not outright philosophical. The gist, as you’ll see below, is that HR will become more effective when it deploys AI to handle the laborious, tedious process stuff so that practitioners can focus on their invaluable human judgment to actual human beings.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">“We don’t need HR built for the industrial era,” says Kristy Kim, CEO of TomoCredit, which uses AI to help people be more literate about their credit score and get help improving it. “When AI handles documentation, onboarding, training, recruiting, and performance insights, the competitive advantage shifts to culture, judgment, incentives, and exceptional talent density.” The future of people teams, she says, lies in designing high-performance organizations built for humans and AI working in concert and “getting out of the way for them to thrive.”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Kim&#8217;s vision is provocative and compelling, but here’s the thing: Many tasks are both exhaustingly tedious and strategic. Prospective employees may represent the most actively engaged consumer segment your company ever encounters, and it doesn’t exactly burnish your brand to alienate 99.9% of them. You don&#8217;t have to <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/hiring" title="Hiring">hire</a> them to retain their respect. You don’t even have to interview them.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Just don’t treat them as merely an agglomeration of keywords whose only job is to tickle your hiring algo.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-hr-works-for-the-business">‘HR works for the business’</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Nobody has to collect too many paychecks before realizing, warm and fuzzy rhetoric aside, HR works for the enterprise. Though people frequently forget. &#8220;People assume that HR has all this power and that they are controlling lots of things,” says Colleen McCreary, chief people officer and head of internal systems at <a href="https://www.confluent.io/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Confluent</a>, the real-time data streaming platform IBM acquired earlier this year for $11 billion. “If only HR did X, Y, and Z, my work life would be better, I would have more money, I would have everything I want. The misconception still exists that HR works for the employee.”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">But business, of course, depends on its workforce to succeed, and in occupying the middle ground between management and labor, HR has the tricky task of negotiating that back and forth. “HR is the balancing point between management and employees,” says Liz Petersen, SHRM’s HR knowledge center manager. “We are not in charge of communication and culture, but we are the facilitators of that.”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Taylor, the SHRM CEO, believes companies have exacerbated employee confusion on this point by adopting the chief people officer title, which signals to employees that their org represents them. “Part of the frustration is that both sides believe you represent them,” he says. “I’m a lawyer by training, and it’s very clear who my client is, and I owe that client my full loyalty. Everyone knows that’s my job. Our frustration comes from having to serve both of these client groups.”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Employees see this, and they resent it. What their negative attitudes toward HR reflect, ultimately, is a crisis of confidence and trust in their employer. Unicorn talents may be immune from the fundamental power imbalance that favors the party who signs their paychecks, but the vast majority of rank-and-file workers know how vulnerable they are to whatever adjustments a CEO decides to make—which last year included nearly 1.2 million layoffs in the U.S.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">“There are only two things that represent an existential threat to human beings,” says Taylor. “One is the threat to their lives, and the second is a threat to their livelihood.&#8221;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Top executives know their employees are stressed. Many also appear to feel the HR pendulum has swung too far towards management. As previously mentioned, 61% of the CEOs who responded to our query believe that HR needs to focus more strategically on acquiring, developing, and retaining talent.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">“HR will never fully earn employee trust if it is also seen as the department that manages risk, discipline, and layoffs,” says Philip Samuelraj, CEO of the AI software company Techjays. To address HR’s divided loyalties, he advocates for “creating a separate Culture Team whose only job is to care for employees, build trust, strengthen belonging, and help people thrive.”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Below, note how many of our leaders’ ideas harken back to the brief period of corporate social enlightenment that gathered momentum during the first Trump administration; prompted the <a href="https://www.businessroundtable.org/business-roundtable-redefines-the-purpose-of-a-corporation-to-promote-an-economy-that-serves-all-americans" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Business Roundtable</a> to issue its 2019 statement redefining the purpose of a corporation as one serving all stakeholders, not just shareholders; and further accelerated during the early days of the pandemic and the Black Lives Matter movement.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Then came the backlash, with HR often the messenger sent to silence the empowered employee.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">With some CEOs still emphasizing employee mental health, highly flexible workplaces, and tailoring company offices (if there are any) to support disabled and neurodivergent employees, it’s worth considering whether the retrenchment from that proworker moment has created a strategic opportunity for those who still see value in many of its ideas.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Or, perhaps there’s a more straightforward solution to management getting the control it wants, employees being okay with it, and HR not being stuck in the middle. “You can’t just tell people they’re appreciated. You have to show them,&#8221; says Charles Stanton, CEO of the Animal Rights Foundation, who earned his HR certification during the pandemic. “We had to take a hard look at our compensation structure and our benefits package, since no amount of coffee and snacks makes up for substandard wages.”</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-give-people-useful-feedback-closer-to-real-time">‘Give people useful feedback closer to real time’</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Once upon a time, organizations had an entire class of employees responsible for nurturing talent and sharing best practices. They were called managers. But the <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91548285/everyone-wants-to-kill-the-middle-manager-role-the-data-says-dont-do-it?utm_source=facebook&amp;utm_medium=social" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Great Flattening</a> has decimated the ranks of middle management. According to a survey conducted by Gartner, as reported by the <em><a href="https://www.wsj.com/business/boss-management-cuts-careers-workplace-4809d750" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Wall Street Journal</a></em>, the median number of direct reports per corporate manager rose from five in 2017 to 15 by 2023, a year when middle managers accounted for <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91548285/everyone-wants-to-kill-the-middle-manager-role-the-data-says-dont-do-it?utm_source=facebook&amp;utm_medium=social" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">31%</a> of corporate layoffs. AI, of course, is accelerating this trend, as my colleague <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91531056/the-middle-managers-ai-survival-guide?oly_enc_id=1772A4187445F5F&amp;r=1772A4187445F5F" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Shalene Gupta recently reported</a>.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">SHRM’s Petersen, the HR knowledge center manager, believes the hollowing out of middle management has made it much harder for employees to get clarity and direction. She compares the challenges of effective middle management to those of the sandwich generation, caring simultaneously for kids and parents. “A middle manager has to be responsible for getting their work up to the executive team but also make sure that every employee reporting to them is doing their job. That’s a difficult place to be.” While executives focus exclusively on strategy, she says, middle managers have to align day-to-day tactical operations with broader corporate directives.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Many of the CEOs we queried view talent development as a key area where HR should be offering strategic value to the organization. “The best HR teams can help grow leaders, make meaningful connections, and create an environment where people can do their best work,” says Jarrod Lopiccolo, CEO of <a href="https://noblestudios.com/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Noble Studios</a>, an integrated <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/marketing" title="Marketing">marketing</a> agency that has worked with the likes of Adobe, Autodesk, and the University of California system.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">But that suggestion should not be interpreted as an endorsement for HR to spend more time on its primary tool for talent development: the dreaded (by employees) performance review.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A very healthy majority of executives (66%, according to the <a href="https://www.forbes.com/sites/carolinecastrillon/2025/06/25/5-reasons-outdated-performance-reviews-make-top-talent-quit/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Acorn 2025 Corporate Performance and Learning Survey</a>) believe their performance review process is great, while only 19% of workers agree. Workers <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91470690/workers-might-hate-performance-reviews-less-if-they-looked-like-this" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">view these reviews</a> as time-consuming, inaccurate, and biased.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;HR should own the performance management process, but not the bureaucratic, once-a-year version most companies run,&#8221; says Sherlock, the organizational strategist. &#8220;The job is to give people useful feedback closer to real time.&#8221; Which means partnering with front-line managers to allow some latitude for how different teams function.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">“AI has made real-time performance management possible, giving managers the ability to coach, align goals, and recognize great work as it happens—not months later,” says Doug Dennerline, who (full disclosure) is CEO of the performance management platform company Betterworks. If not done carefully, though, continuous performance assessment risks veering into <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11300163/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">workplace surveillance</a> and transforming the semiannual or annual inconvenience into the workplace equivalent of a daily root canal.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For some business leaders, the question is not how—or how often—workers are evaluated but what companies are assessing. “We treat employees like machines that need optimization, ignoring that creativity requires emotional rest,” says Volen Vulkov, cofounder of the résumé-builder company <a href="https://enhancv.com/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Enhancv</a>. “HR should aggressively audit organizational fatigue, tracking calendar bloat and digital noise. When HR protects a team’s collective peace as a sacred corporate asset, exceptional performance follows naturally.”</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-ai-human-intelligence-the-new-roi">‘AI + Human Intelligence = the new ROI’</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Many of us are experiencing sleepless nights worrying about how long it’ll be before AI consumes our jobs (or maybe worrying whether <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91549044/my-ai-night-shift" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">the agentic tools we’ve created</a> are working or wreaking havoc). This anxiety is a variation on the age-old fear of tech displacing workers.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">As AI permeates every aspect of our working lives, how do we adapt? Whether you call it human resources, people operations, or something else, how does the function retain some humanity?</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">SHRM’s Taylor proposes a new equation: Artificial Intelligence + Human Intelligence = the new ROI. &#8220;Artificial intelligence complementing and supplementing human intelligence,&#8221; he says. &#8220;When AI is making the hiring decision, that is very, very bad.” The formula sounds a little too cute, but isn’t that the way all of us are living every day right now?</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">“You need people looking at résumés again. You need people interviewing again. Prescreening needs to be more human,&#8221; adds ForHumanity’s Pederson, saying job candidates need &#8220;touchpoints that aren’t chatbots.”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Nifty mathematical-looking equations aside, the balancing of human intelligence and AI can be tricky, as my colleague Pavithra Mohan illustrated in her recent investigation into Amazon’s experiment of <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91565321/amazon-is-taking-human-out-of-hr-ai-chatbot-app-aza" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">replacing HR practitioners with AI</a> in one of its Ohio warehouses.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">To truly retain an element of humanity, HR practitioners have to become more effective advocates of human workers. “So many HR teams fall down because they get into touchy-feely conversations,” says Confluent’s McCreary, “instead of saying these are the reasons why investing in employees is important . . . using that same business lens that any other operator at the table would be expected to use.”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Edgar Villanueva, founder and CEO of the <a href="https://www.decolonizingwealth.com/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Decolonizing Wealth Project</a>, a global nonprofit, summarizes this view quite succinctly: “Care for your people like your business depends on it.”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Because it does.</p>

<h3 class="wp-block-heading">Redefine HR’s role in the organization</h3>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">“Many HR functions have gotten better at measuring and attempting to improve organizational health, and that work matters. But <strong>the bigger opportunity is strategic readiness: helping leaders build the talent, leadership, culture, structure, and adaptability required for what comes next.</strong> The best HR teams should not only assess whether the organization is healthy today, but actively drive whether it is becoming more capable, resilient, and future-ready.”—<strong>R. Ethan Braden, chief marketing and communications officer, Texas A&amp;M University</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">“The years went by, and HR’s overreaching interventionist style, often resonating less as policy than as social engineering, did not lose strength. The middle-management mindset still obstructs talent and creates models that make little sense for competing teams. <strong>Having spent time in sports, I believe it could offer HR a way out.</strong> Formula 1 builds around elite drivers, basketball around franchise players, and tennis around individualized teams for top performers. Sports understand selection, merit, training, hierarchy, discipline, and the brutal visibility of results.
