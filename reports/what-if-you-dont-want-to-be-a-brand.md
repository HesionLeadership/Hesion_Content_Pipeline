# What if you don’t want to be a brand?

**Source:** Fast Company  
**Published:** 2026-07-30T09:15:00  
**URL:** [https://www.fastcompany.com/91573506/what-if-you-dont-want-to-be-a-brand-career-advice-personal-brand](https://www.fastcompany.com/91573506/what-if-you-dont-want-to-be-a-brand-career-advice-personal-brand)

## Summary
The article explores the growing pressure on professionals to build personal brands, arguing that this trend—amplified by AI commodifying competence—risks reducing human complexity, curiosity, and growth into curated, machine-like personas.

## Organizational Psychology Angle
The tension between personal branding and authentic professional identity maps onto research on identity work in organizations, where employees navigate the conflict between externally imposed role expectations and their evolving sense of self. The pressure to maintain a fixed professional brand can constrain role exploration and psychological growth, undermining intrinsic motivation and long-term career adaptability.

## Leadership Lesson
CHROs and COOs should be cautious about encouraging employees to develop rigid personal brands as a development strategy. Instead, foster organizational cultures that reward curiosity, skill diversification, and career reinvention—recognizing that the capacity for authentic growth and exploration may be a more durable competitive advantage than a polished personal narrative, especially as AI commodifies routine competence.

## Original Story Summary
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Not long ago, a career was something you built. Today, it increasingly feels like something you package. Find your niche. Own your lane. Build an audience. Create content. Become discoverable. Stay visible. Stay relevant.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Somewhere between LinkedIn headlines, newsletters, podcasts, personal websites, and carefully curated online personas, many professionals inherited a second job: becoming their own <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/marketing" title="Marketing">marketing</a> department.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The rise of <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/artificial-intelligence" title="AI">artificial intelligence</a> may only intensify the pressure.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For years, we feared AI would make human workers obsolete. Instead, AI appears to be changing the rules of differentiation. If a machine can produce competent emails, competent presentations, competent reports, competent code, and increasingly competent ideas, then competence itself becomes easier to find and harder to distinguish. The question is no longer simply, &#8220;<em>What can you do?</em>&#8221; Increasingly, the question becomes, &#8220;<em>Why should anyone remember that you did it?</em>&#8220;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The market&#8217;s answer seems clear: Become memorable. Become recognizable. Become searchable. Become a brand.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Younger workers appear particularly aware of these incentives. <a href="https://intel.morningconsult.com/mc-content/analysis/personal-brands-important-gen-z-2024?utm_source=chatgpt.com">Research</a> has found that 67% of Gen Z adults believe having a strong personal brand is important, while 61% either already have one or are actively interested in developing one. It’s been found that 61% of <a href="https://intel.morningconsult.com/mc-content/analysis/personal-brands-important-gen-z-2024?utm_source=chatgpt.com">Gen Z workers</a> expect employers to provide personal <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/branding" title="Branding">branding</a> tools and training as an employee benefit. Personal branding is no longer viewed as a side project. Increasingly, it is viewed as part of the job itself.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The labor market reinforces the lesson. According to <a href="https://www.careerbuilder.com?utm_source=chatgpt.com">CareerBuilder</a>, 47% of employers report being less likely to interview candidates they cannot find online, while 70% use social media to research applicants during the <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/hiring" title="Hiring">hiring</a> process. Invisibility, it turns out, can be interpreted as a lack of ambition, relevance, or engagement rather than simply privacy.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Workers are responding rationally to the incentives in front of them.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The problem is that brands are designed to reduce ambiguity while human beings are designed to accumulate it. A successful brand answers questions quickly. What does this company do? Who is it for? Why should anyone care? The clearer and more consistent the answers, the stronger the brand becomes. Human lives rarely work that way.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-professional-twists">Professional twists</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Most of us become more complicated with age, not less. We collect interests that don&#8217;t fit together neatly. We take detours. We discover passions that would have surprised our younger selves. The management professor becomes interested in fiction. The engineer becomes fascinated by theology. The entrepreneur discovers that mentoring young employees is more fulfilling than building another company.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Professionally, these twists are often where our best ideas come from. They are evidence of curiosity, reinvention, and growth. Yet they are precisely the kinds of things a branding expert might advise us to edit out in favor of a clearer narrative and a more coherent market position.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Somewhere along the way, many professionals stopped asking, <em>&#8220;What do I want to become?&#8221;</em> and started asking, <em>&#8220;What do I want to become known for?&#8221;</em></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The difference between those questions is larger than it first appears.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The first assumes that identity is unfinished and that careers are places we travel through. The second assumes that identity is a destination to be selected, defended, and communicated consistently to an audience. One invites exploration. The other rewards commitment. One assumes there may be value in becoming someone unexpected. The other encourages us to become increasingly efficient versions of who we already are.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-people-are-not-products">People are not products</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">There is another danger lurking beneath the pressure to become a brand: Eventually we begin treating ourselves like products. Experiences become content opportunities. Hobbies become side hustles. Conversations become posts. Vacations become material. Life slowly transforms from something we experience into something we curate.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Eventually we stop asking whether we are enjoying our lives and start asking whether our lives are performing well.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The irony is difficult to ignore.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">At precisely the moment artificial intelligence is making distinctly human qualities more valuable—judgment, empathy, humor, trust, curiosity, mentorship, creativity, and moral reasoning—professional culture is encouraging many of us to become more machine-like ourselves: optimized, curated, predictable, and algorithmically legible.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We worried AI would become human.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Instead, many humans are becoming brands.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The pressure isn&#8217;t limited to younger workers trying to establish themselves. One well-known leadership expert told me anonymously, &#8220;I probably shouldn&#8217;t say this, but sometimes I&#8217;m just over it. I&#8217;m tired of being &#8216;that guy.&#8217; My interests are all over the place, but most people would never know it because that&#8217;s not what the market wants from me. Sometimes I wonder what I&#8217;m sacrificing in order to keep being this brand.&#8221;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">His comment stayed with me because it captured something larger than burnout or boredom.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Brands are valuable because they create familiarity. They create trust. They make us easier to find, easier to understand, and easier to remember. But they also create expectations. Eventually the challenge stops being building the brand and starts becoming living inside it. The danger isn&#8217;t simply that we become brands. The danger is that we become loyal to versions of ourselves we have already outgrown.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Careers are supposed to evolve. Interests are supposed to drift. Human beings are supposed to surprise themselves occasionally. The ability to become someone new may turn out to be one of the last competitive advantages that cannot be automated, outsourced, or optimized away. It would be a shame if we surrendered it voluntarily. Because perhaps the real question facing professionals in the age of AI isn&#8217;t whether we can build a successful personal brand. Perhaps it is whether we can build one without allowing it to become our identity.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">After all, a brand asks us to remain recognizable. A human life asks something much more difficult: the freedom to become someone our audience—and perhaps even ourselves—never expected.</p>
