## Original Story Summary
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Being the always reliable team member at work can earn trust—but it can also limit your growth and cause burnout. Below, experts share how to challenge unfair expectations, set clear boundaries, and avoid becoming the only person who can keep things running. Learn when to share responsibility, seek career mobility, and move beyond a role that depends on constant overwork.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-share-knowledge-before-dependency-traps-you">Share Knowledge Before Dependency Traps You</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Being needed feels like winning. It isn&#8217;t. When a company depends on you so much that your absence becomes a liability, that&#8217;s not job security. That&#8217;s a company that has become vulnerable to you, and you&#8217;ve become vulnerable to it.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">You built the thing. You carry the wisdom. People genuinely need your skill. Early in a career that&#8217;s exactly how you should be operating: useful, visible, willing to take on what&#8217;s unfamiliar.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">But the moment your knowledge stays sealed inside you instead of getting shared, documented, or taught, valuable turns into what risk management calls key person risk. The business stops seeing your expertise as an asset and starts seeing you as its biggest exposure. This isn&#8217;t rare.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://hbr.org/2016/01/collaborative-overload">Research published in the Harvard Business Review</a> found that 20% to 35% of an organization&#8217;s value-added collaboration comes from just 3% to 5% of employees—the ones everyone routes to. Those same employees had the lowest engagement and career satisfaction scores of anyone measured. It isn&#8217;t noble to be the one person a system can&#8217;t run without. It&#8217;s a countdown.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">An example from my career: One engineer built a core piece of our operating system almost entirely on his own. It became something the business depended on daily. He never translated it into anything anyone else could run. No documentation, no standard process, no one else who understood the internals. When he went on vacation, the system would break and someone would have to track him down to fix it. That wasn&#8217;t heroic. That was a business betting its operations on one person&#8217;s memory, and neither side came out ahead.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Here&#8217;s what actually makes you indispensable in a way that helps your career: sharing the knowledge, training your replacement, and releasing what you know instead of hoarding it. That&#8217;s how you grow. Staying the only one who knows isn&#8217;t job security. It&#8217;s how you get stuck.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/lenamcdearmid">Lena McDearmid</a>, CEO and founder, <a href="https://wryver.com">Wryver</a></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-beware-roles-that-stall-advancement">Beware Roles That Stall Advancement</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">One hidden downside of becoming the reliable one is that your reliability can eventually become the reason an organization keeps you exactly where you are.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I experienced this firsthand as a facilitator. My life circumstances gave me unusual flexibility. I could travel on short notice, arrive early, stay longer, and give clients my full attention. I loved the work and consistently gave it everything. Over time I was regularly partnered with colleagues whom leadership knew would contribute significantly less because, as it was made clear to me, I would pick up the slack. And I did. There was no additional compensation for carrying more of the work. My reliability simply became expected.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Clients eventually began requesting me by name, which brought more work and responsibility but not more opportunity. When I inquired about moving into a more advanced role, I was essentially told I was too valuable where I already was and that moving me would hurt that part of the business.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That experience taught me not to confuse being indispensable with being developed. They are not the same thing. Organizations should be careful that their most reliable employees are not rewarded with everyone else&#8217;s unfinished work while their own growth gets postponed.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/iamjustinkey">Justin D. Key</a>, storyteller, scholar, strategist, <a href="https://www.blacktheorem.com">Black Theorem Co</a>.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-confront-leaders-who-exploit-competence">Confront Leaders Who Exploit Competence</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;You&#8217;re so good at this, can you also . . . ?&#8221; This statement isn&#8217;t about YOUR performance as the reliable one. It&#8217;s about THEIR weak leadership and the fact that they were titled above their competence level—and a pathological desire on the part of too many leaders to avoid having courageous conversations. Leaders lean on their strongest workers because they don&#8217;t want to confront the underperformers. They&#8217;ve decided that your &#8220;loyalty&#8221;—and personal desire to deliver great work—is easier to absorb than having an unpleasant conversation with underperformers.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Healthy cultures treat capacity as a strategic constraint to manage, not an individual weakness to override with guilt or flattery. In a weak-leadership culture, accountability is risky, so leaders assign work to the people they know can do it.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I was asked to do the work a tenured Chief Strategy Officer was incapable of doing—rigor, communication, and action—to capture sentiment and build processes/systems around meaningful work getting done. The teams/task forces this CSO sponsored would send sugary-sweet tropes to me via Slack, expecting me to do the diagnostic work they were incapable of capturing and building a plan around. It became the norm, but here&#8217;s the truly icky part: When you&#8217;re the go-to person, your competence becomes the baseline for your work (it&#8217;s no longer &#8220;exceeds expectations&#8221;—it&#8217;s &#8220;meets&#8221;). Your effort becomes unseen because it&#8217;s what&#8217;s expected of you. Result: People who don&#8217;t perform ride on the backs of truly talented individuals.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">So the downside is you&#8217;re feeding this culture of incompetence while your competence is taken for granted and taken advantage of.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/bethboyd">Elizabeth Boyd</a>, global director, organizational development &amp; strategy, <a href="https://talentlab.live">TalentLab.Live</a></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-reward-ownership-with-career-mobility">Reward Ownership With Career Mobility</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We had a team member who was hired as an inside sales representative. However, he had been with the company for a long time and understood IT and how the company operated early on. Because he was so reliable and knew so much, he became the go-to person across multiple departments: customer service, IT, finance, and sales. He was constantly being pulled in every direction, creating Excel documents and configuring automated reports. He didn&#8217;t want to let anyone down, so he continued to say yes and take on more responsibility while A work became C work. His own professional growth slowed. <br /><br />The company became so dependent on him and his institutional knowledge, even when the work was no longer consistent, that they developed a business risk by allowing one person to become the solution. That&#8217;s a problem. A company needs to continue to evolve and add A players and give other people ownership of their roles. My personal leadership lesson: Being the reliable person can become a disadvantage when reliability is rewarded with more work instead of more opportunity.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/alexsandra-sisler-b34b3379">AlexSandra Sisler</a>, keynote speaker &amp; retail + aesthetics executive, <a href="https://www.alexsandrasisler.com">EmpowerServe</a></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-disperse-critical-controls-to-reduce-exposure">Disperse Critical Controls to Reduce Exposure</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The hidden downside is that reliability concentrates risk. When one person becomes the answer to every urgent question, the organization stops building the systems that would replace them. That person becomes a single point of failure.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I see this through the lens of fraud risk and intangible asset valuation. Knowledge that lives in one person&#8217;s head is an asset the company does not really own. Investors discount it as key person risk, and they are right to. If the go-to person leaves or burns out, the value walks out with them.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">There is a second, even less comfortable downside. Unchecked trust removes oversight. Colleagues stop questioning the person they rely on most, because asking feels like doubting them.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The most extreme public example is QuadrigaCX, the Canadian crypto exchange. The founder, Gerald Cotten, reportedly held sole control of the company&#8217;s funds and passwords. When he died suddenly in 2018, tens of thousands of customers could not recover their money. The Ontario Securities Commission later found he had been misusing client funds for years. Shared controls would likely have exposed that much earlier.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Most go-to people are honest, of course. The lesson is structural. Heavy reliance on one person strains them and exposes the organization, whether or not anything goes wrong.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">What leaders can do:</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">• Pair every critical task with a documented backup owner.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">• Rotate responsibilities so knowledge spreads.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">• Require dual approval for anything touching money or client data.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">• Watch workload, because the most dependable people rarely ask for relief.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Protecting your reliable people means making sure the company could run for a week without them.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/sameersomal">Sameer Somal</a>, cofounder, <a href="https://www.legalexperts.ai">Legal Experts AI</a></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-set-boundaries-before-capacity-collapses">Set Boundaries Before Capacity Collapses</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">One hidden downside of always being the go-to person is that your reliability can quietly turn into other people&#8217;s default plan, which steadily drains your capacity until decision-making and focus suffer. In my coaching work, I often see this show up when delegation becomes a &#8220;dump and run,&#8221; where work is handed off without clarity on scope, deliverables, bandwidth, or resources. The reliable person says yes, carries the ambiguity, and ends up with too many &#8220;apps running in the background&#8221; that sap energy even when the task list looks manageable. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Over time, that constant load can create resentment and fatigue, not because the person cannot do the work, but because the expectations were never discussed and agreed to. It also removes agency, initiative, and innovation from others. If I&#8217;m always the one who gets things done then I&#8217;m training those around me to not be. I&#8217;m also training the organization to just give me more to do. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I have a client I coach that gets rewarded for being dependable and performing. They get rewarded with more work. They haven&#8217;t verbalized their goals or aspirations to the organization. They might want more challenges and new skills, but that is not always more of the same work. <br /><br />The organization will give you more of what you&#8217;re good at and create stagnation in your own career path because they can&#8217;t afford for you to leave that critical position. It is a dangerous corner to paint yourself into. Being valued and being critical in a position are two different things.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/janice-perkins">Janice Perkins</a>, executive coach, <a href="https://www.ihavecapacity.com">Capacity Communications</a></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-build-leaders-beyond-the-lone-wolf">Build Leaders Beyond the Lone Wolf</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">As a 20-year operator who previously led North America&#8217;s largest entrepreneurial mental health platform (#REALTALK), I&#8217;d say the downside of being the &#8220;reliable one&#8221; is that it inhibits the creation of a culture where everyone is a leader. When someone is labeled as the go-to person, it implies that others in the company are relying on this person to drive the result (e.g., sales), rather than the ownership of that result being split among team members.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">What often occurs is that the go-to person is deemed a high performer, but will often encounter severe burnout later in their career. In some extreme cases, being the go-to person can coincide with being a &#8220;lone wolf,&#8221; feeling the pressure of having to deliver the result yourself and telling yourself the story that &#8220;you are the only one who can do this.&#8221;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Over time, teams and companies are unable to expand because it is not possible to have scale (by delegating and cultivating the next generation of leaders), when that leadership and performance is hyperlocalized to just a few individuals.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In my work with 45,000 leaders, we find that being the reliable one has consequences:</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">• Not being able to shut off from work: 56% of tech professionals can&#8217;t relax once their workday is over, and 62% feel physically and emotionally drained. Source: Yerbo (2022).</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">• Not being able to share or ask for help: 81% of founders hide their stress, fears, and challenges from others, and more than half hide their stress from their own cofounders. Source: Startup Snapshot (2023).</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">• Women being disproportionately affected: Women are 44% more likely to be asked to volunteer on low-level tasks that need to be done (but don&#8217;t advance your career—e.g., taking notes, serving on committees), and women were 50% more likely to comply with requests to volunteer. Source: University of Melbourne (2017).</p>
