# The invisible tax that might be holding back your startup

**Source:** Fast Company  
**Published:** 2026-07-26T05:00:00  
**URL:** [https://www.fastcompany.com/91577885/the-invisible-tax-that-might-be-holding-back-your-startup](https://www.fastcompany.com/91577885/the-invisible-tax-that-might-be-holding-back-your-startup)

## Summary
Co-founder relationship tension acts as a hidden 'tax' on startups, degrading decision speed, decision quality, and team engagement in ways that compound over time but are rarely recognized as a core business risk.

## Organizational Psychology Angle
The article illustrates how top management team (TMT) relational dynamics function as an upstream determinant of organizational effectiveness—misalignment at the top cascades through decision-making processes, employee engagement, and execution quality, consistent with upper echelons theory and research on TMT behavioral integration.

## Leadership Lesson
CHROs and COOs should treat the co-founder or C-suite relationship as a critical operating system requiring regular structured maintenance—standing check-ins on role clarity, accountability, and mutual needs—rather than assuming senior leaders will privately resolve interpersonal friction before it becomes an organizational drag.

The only limitation is that it's practitioner-authored rather than empirically tested, but the org psych relevance is excellent.

## Original Story Summary
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In most companies, tension between co-founders is treated as a personal issue. It’s uncomfortable, it may affect morale, and in extreme cases, it can become a governance concern, but it is rarely treated as a core business risk. I believe that is a mistake.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In high-growth companies, the co-founder relationship is the company’s primary decision-making system. When that system is strained, the organization absorbs it, not just emotionally, but also and perhaps even more importantly—operationally and financially.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">What follows is a hidden but measurable cost: slower decisions, degraded judgment, disengaged teams, and missed opportunities. And because it rarely appears on a balance sheet, it is often allowed to compound unchecked.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I&#8217;ve coached founders for over a decade through high-growth, high-pressure moments. I get a front-row seat to the relationship dynamics that either accelerate or erode company performance in these moments. I&#8217;ve repeatedly observed that when the system comes under strain, the consequences can include lost revenue and <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/productivity" title="Productivity">productivity</a>, reduced valuation, and, in severe cases, company failure.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-the-misdiagnosis-problem">The Misdiagnosis Problem</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">When companies begin to slow down or feel harder to run, leaders typically look for structural explanations. They point to unclear roles, communication breakdowns, performance gaps and perhaps cultural misalignment. Those diagnoses are not wrong but they are often incomplete.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In many cases, these issues are downstream of something more fundamental: the people at the top of the company are no longer able to execute clearly together under pressure.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The shift is subtle at first. Conversations take longer. Decisions require more energy. Alignment becomes harder to reach. Over time, what was once a high-functioning partnership becomes a source of physical and emotional friction that the rest of the organization must navigate. At that point, the company is paying a tax whether it recognizes it or not.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-the-three-layers-of-the-co-founder-relationship-tax">The Three Layers of the Co-Founder Relationship Tax</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The impact of co-founder tension cascades through the organization in three distinct ways: time, energy, and decision quality.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>1. The Time Tax</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">When co-founders are aligned, decisions tend to be fast, clear, and durable. When they are not, decisions slow down and often have to be revisited or reversed/remade. Leaders re-discuss the same issues in multiple forums. They draw in more people, seeking additional validation. They delay committing, or commit only partially. Conversations that once took 20 minutes between a select few people now take an hour, or happen repeatedly across different settings with additional people. This is expensive.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Senior leaders already spend an enormous portion of their time in meetings:<a href="https://hbr.org/2017/07/stop-the-meeting-madness"> nearly 23 hours per week</a>. <a href="https://www.mckinsey.com/~/media/mckinsey/business%20functions/people%20and%20organizational%20performance/our%20insights/decision%20making%20in%20the%20age%20of%20urgency/decision-making-in-the-age-of-urgency.pdf?shouldIndex=false">McKinsey research</a> found that executives spend 37% of their time on decision-making, and more than half of that time is used ineffectively—translating, at a typical large company, to more than 530,000 lost working days and $250 million in wasted labor costs per year. In a growth-stage company with a six-person executive team, even modest decision friction can translate into hundreds of thousands of dollars in lost executive capacity annually.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>2. The Energy and Engagement Tax</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The effects of founder tension do not stay contained at the top. They spread, and quickly. Leadership teams are highly sensitive to signals from founders. When co-founders are not fully aligned (at best) or visibly combative, those signals become mixed. Teams begin to hedge. They double-check decisions. They escalate more often. They become cautious where they were once decisive. They feel they are serving two masters instead of one, and they can’t be sure those two masters even want the same outcome. Moreover, energy gets redirected from their jobs to reading and managing their cofounders’ emotions. Over time, this creates a subtle but pervasive shift: people stop moving as quickly or as confidently as they once did.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This is not simply a matter of morale. It affects engagement, and engagement has well-documented economic consequences. Data from Gallup shows that employees who are not engaged cost their company <a href="https://www.gallup.com/workplace/321743/increase-productivity-lowest-possible-cost.aspx">the equivalent of 18% of their annual salary</a>. Manager quality is one of the<a href="https://news.gallup.com/businessjournal/182792/managers-account-variance-employee-engagement.aspx"> primary drivers of engagement</a>, which means that instability at the top disproportionately affects how teams perform. Even a small number of partially disengaged employees can create a meaningful financial drag. More importantly, disengagement compounds. As uncertainty increases, communication becomes more guarded, initiative declines, and execution slows.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>3. The Decision Quality Tax</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The most significant cost of all is also the hardest to measure. When co-founders are under strain, their ability to access their full intelligence—analytical, emotional, and intuitive—narrows. They become more reactive, more rigid, or more avoidant. They over-index on certain types of information while discounting others. In this state, decisions are not only slower but legitimately worse<strong>. </strong>Research from Bain &amp; Company has shown that <a href="https://media.bain.com/Images/DECISION%20INSIGHTS_Compendium_Issues1-5.pdf">companies that make fast, high-quality decisions outperform their peers</a>.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The inverse is also true: when decision quality degrades, performance suffers, often dramatically. In high-growth environments, the cost of a single misaligned decision can be enormous: a delayed <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/hiring" title="Hiring">hire</a>, a missed market window, a poorly sequenced product bet. These are not incremental losses. They are compounding ones. This is where the co-founder relationship tax becomes most dangerous. It is no longer just a matter of wasted time or reduced productivity. It is threatening to the company’s life force.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-why-the-tax-goes-unaddressed">Why the Tax Goes Unaddressed</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">If the costs are so significant, why do companies fail to address them? One reason is that the symptoms are misdiagnosed. Leaders see slower execution, but attribute it to process. They see misalignment, but attribute it to communication. They see disengagement, but attribute it to personal reasons. In fact, founders often give themselves better marks on communicating than the team gives them, and they&#8217;re more likely to hope a person&#8217;s disengagement is due to a bad day than culture, which is a much bigger fix.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Another reason is that co-founder relationships are often treated as private. There is an implicit assumption that founders should “work it out” themselves, or that addressing relational dynamics is outside the scope of normal business operations. Paradoxically, co-founders think they&#8217;re sparing the team by managing their issues privately when the conflict is actually visible to the team.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Additionally, many leaders lack a clear framework for understanding how relational strain translates into business outcomes. Without that connection, the problem is hard to bring up, hard to make tangible, and therefore easy to deprioritize.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-reducing-the-tax">Reducing the Tax</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Addressing the co-founder relationship tax does not require eliminating tension. In many cases, some degree of tension is productive. What matters is whether co-founders can maintain access to clear thinking and shared reality under pressure. In practice, this involves three shifts:</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>1. Making the relationship a business priority</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Treat the co-founder relationship as a core operating system, not a side concern. This means investing time and attention into each founder understanding themselves and each other, and subsequently, <em>how</em> decisions are made, not just what decisions are made. Founders often become aware of the need to make this a priority through conversations with other trusted founders who have been there, investors who notice warning signs, valuable employees who call out the dysfunction/threaten to quit, and occasionally articles like this where something clicks with the unease they feel but couldn’t pinpoint and triggers a conversation with their co-founders. And at times, founders take the initiative to proactively do this after a previous venture failed due to a breakdown in the co-founder partnership. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>2. Increasing visibility and truth-telling</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Create conditions where misalignment can be surfaced early, before it becomes structural. This often requires explicit norms around candor, disagreement, and repair. Example: one of my clients has a standing agenda item at their bi-monthly in-person retreat where they dig into a particular aspect of their partnership: one month they might discuss whether their roles are still clear and make sense. Another month: Is it still clear who is accountable for what? Another: What does each founder need more or less of from each other? (For this last one, I require them to come up with something substantial; they can’t take the easy way out). This routine ensures potential alignment issues are surfaced, and encourages mutual honesty.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>3. Restoring decision integrity under pressure</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Developing the capacity, individually and together, to stay integrated when stakes are high. This includes recognizing when thinking has narrowed and deliberately expanding it. For this, I help teams understand how to recognize the unproductive stories we tell ourselves (this is when our thinking narrows in response to stress) and reframe thoughts to support our goals (this is how we deliberately expand it). </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">These are ongoing practices, not one-time interventions.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-the-cost-of-ignoring-strained-relationships">The Cost of Ignoring Strained Relationships</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Companies rarely fail because of a single bad decision or a single strained relationship. They fail because small inefficiencies compound over time. The co-founder relationship tax is one of those inefficiencies. It consumes time and energy, and it degrades judgment. And because it is often invisible or seen as unquantifiable, it is allowed to persist longer than it should. In a competitive environment where speed and clarity are critical, that is a risk few companies can afford.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The most effective leaders understand that performance is not just a function of strategy or talent. It is a function of how clearly people can think and act together when it matters most. And that begins at the top.</p>
