# These are the 3 things that are actually burning you out (and only one is your boss)

**Source:** Fast Company  
**Published:** 2026-08-04T10:00:00  
**URL:** [https://www.fastcompany.com/91583115/these-are-the-3-things-that-are-actually-burning-you-out-and-only-one-is-your-boss](https://www.fastcompany.com/91583115/these-are-the-3-things-that-are-actually-burning-you-out-and-only-one-is-your-boss)

## Summary
Burnout stems from three converging cultural forces: (1) societal culture that equates worth with productivity and frames rest as laziness, (2) organizational culture marked by structural overwork, chronic understaffing, and 'hypocrisy culture' (the gap between wellness rhetoric and lived reality), and (3) internalized culture—absorbed beliefs that tie personal worth to achievement. Addressing only one force without the others fails to prevent burnout.

## Organizational Psychology Angle
The article presents a multi-level ecological model of burnout, arguing that organizational interventions (wellness programs, policy changes) fail when they ignore the societal norms that normalize overwork and the internalized beliefs that make individuals complicit in their own depletion. This aligns with research showing that individual-level burnout interventions without structural change produce limited, short-lived effects.

## Leadership Lesson
CHROs and COOs must close the gap between wellness rhetoric and structural reality—audit whether organizational systems (staffing levels, role clarity, reward structures, availability expectations) actually enable recovery, rather than relying on wellness programs that relocate systemic responsibility onto individuals. Additionally, help employees examine internalized overwork beliefs without framing burnout as a personal failing.

## Original Story Summary
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Almost every conversation I have with leaders these days revolves around the topic of burnout in their teams.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">And I’m not talking about having a bad day or a hard week, but the real thing. You know, the kind that creeps in quietly, accumulates over years, and eventually floors you.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The World Health Organization defines burnout as a syndrome caused by <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91377187/how-to-stop-stress-from-hijacking-your-productivity" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">chronic workplace</a> stress that hasn&#8217;t been managed well. <em>Chronic</em>. That word matters. Burnout isn’t a rough quarter or a string of late nights before a big deadline. It is the result of sustained, unrelenting pressure—and it has a specific shape. That looks like deep exhaustion, growing cynicism toward your work, and a growing sense that you’re no longer effective at things you once did well.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Once you understand <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91542334/we-dont-have-a-burnout-epidemic-we-have-a-burnout-buzzword-problem" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">what burnout is</a>, a more useful question opens up: What, precisely, is generating that chronic stress? In my research and in my work with organizations and leaders, I’ve come to see it not as a single-cause problem but as a convergence.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">As I outline in my book <a href="https://www.amazon.com.au/Protect-Your-Spark-Prevent-Authentically-ebook/dp/B09HRC5GVN" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank"><em>Protect Your Spark</em></a>, there are three distinct cultural forces that combine to create a perfect storm of chronic workplace stress. And until we name all three, we won’t fix the root of the problem.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-1-societal-culture">1. Societal culture</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The first force is the broadest and the hardest to see. Long before we enter our first workplace, we’ve already absorbed a dense set of messages about work: what accomplishments mean, what success looks like, and what our value as human beings depends on.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Consider the norms we’ve come to accept as simply &#8220;how things are.&#8221; For example, cultures that frame rest as laziness and overwork as dedication; systems that measure human value through <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/productivity" title="Productivity">productivity</a> and output alone; and the pervasive message that the busiest person in the room is also the most important. We ask children what they want to <em>be</em> when they grow up. But the first thing we ask adults is what they <em>do</em>.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">These aren’t neutral norms. They’re the invisible architecture that we’ve built our working lives on. &nbsp;</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-2-organizational-culture-the-system-closest-to-us">2. Organizational culture: the system closest to us</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The second force is the one we most readily name, because it’s the most visible. And it is real; the culture of the organization in which we work has an enormous influence on <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91552466/burnout-isnt-about-working-too-much" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">our risk of burnout</a>.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Structural overwork, chronic understaffing, unclear or ever-shifting role boundaries, and frequent reorganizations aren’t isolated management failures. They are features of organizations that have inherited, and often amplified, the harmful norms of the societies they sit within. So too is what I think of as&nbsp;<em>hypocrisy culture</em>: the gap between what organizations say (“our people are our greatest asset,” “we care about your wellbeing”) and what they structurally do—reward&nbsp;<a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/90522017/there-but-not-there-is-your-team-suffering-from-pressenteeism" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">presenteeism</a>, normalize round-the-clock availability, and expect fewer people to absorb ever-growing workloads.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In my consulting work, this gap is one of the most consistent burnout drivers I encounter. Leaders often genuinely want their people to thrive. But intention without structural change is noise. No wellness initiative neutralizes a structurally toxic system.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-3-internalized-culture">3. Internalized culture</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The third force is the most personal and what most of us underestimate the most. It is also the one that leaders often use against individuals. I want to be clear: This isn’t about weakness or personal failing. It is about the beliefs we absorb from the two forces above that we mistake them for our own deeply held values.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We are not born believing “I don&#8217;t stop when I&#8217;m tired. I stop when I&#8217;m done,” or “burnout is the price of success,” or “asking for help is weak.” Those are all things we learned. We absorb them from caregivers, peers, education systems, media, and advertising that have an interest—sometimes a financial one—in convincing us that our worth is something we have to earn.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The people most susceptible to this force are often the most driven. Those who have internalized the message that their worth depends on their achievements, and who push hardest and longest as a result. Individual factors like personality and early life experience can shape how each of us absorbs these cultural pressures. But those personal factors are downstream of the cultural ones. They tell us about the kindling, not the fire. Change the culture, and you change the risk landscape for everyone.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-the-importance-of-naming-all-three">The importance of naming all three</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Here is what this means in practice. If an organization rolls out a wellness program but leaves structural overwork intact, it has done nothing except relocate responsibility to individuals. If we support people in examining their internalized beliefs but never interrogate the societal norms that installed them, we’re asking them to swim upstream indefinitely. And if we reform organizational policy without addressing the broader cultural forces at play, we end up with islands of relative health in an otherwise unchanged sea.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Real prevention requires honesty about all three forces at once. At a societal level, that means challenging the norms we perpetuate about rest, worth, and what a meaningful life looks like. When it comes to the organizational level, it means structural accountability—closing the gap between wellness rhetoric and lived reality. At an individual level, it means giving people genuine frameworks to examine what they have internalized, not to burden them with responsibility for a systemic problem, but to help them understand the full terrain they’re navigating.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Burnout results from unmanaged chronic stress. Successfully managing it means going to its roots: the society that normalizes overwork, the organization that perpetuates it, and the beliefs we have each internalized—without fault, without intention—that make us complicit in our own depletion.</p>
