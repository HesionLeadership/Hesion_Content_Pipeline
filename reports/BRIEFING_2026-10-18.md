# Morning Briefing — October 18, 2026

## This Week's Themes
The dominant theme this week is **AI-driven erosion of human capability**—whether it's skill atrophy when AI fails (**What happens when people have to jump in to clean up AI's mess?**, Fast Company), judgment preservation (**The Defining Challenge of Responsible AI Is Designing Systems That Preserve Human Judgment When and Where It Counts**, MIT Sloan Review), or teams losing transactive memory by offloading to AI instead of colleagues (**Team Dynamics Weaken When Employees Turn to AI instead of Colleagues**, HBR). A second theme is **the changing psychological contract between employer and employee**, seen in both the normalization of multiple jobs (**'Parallel work': How holding down multiple jobs at once shed its taboo**, Fast Company) and the question of what durable value looks like when AI makes speed cheap (**Move slow and make things: What Japanese craftsmanship can teach business about quality, patience, and expertise when AI makes speed cheap**, Fortune). A third theme is **trust and accountability design**—from Anthropic ceding employee-level access to external researchers (**Anthropic is letting METR researchers look under the hood with employee-level access**, TechCrunch) to organizational liability questions in **Why companies may be liable for AI errors** (Fast Company). Together, these stories suggest leaders are grappling with how to preserve human expertise and trust structures while AI reshapes both the pace and nature of work.

## Top Story
**Move slow and make things: What Japanese craftsmanship can teach business about quality, patience, and expertise when AI makes speed cheap** (Fortune) is the clear top pick—it's the only story with full article text and offers a concrete counter-narrative to the AI-speed obsession dominating the news cycle. For CHROs and COOs, it reframes the AI conversation: instead of asking "how much faster can we go," ask "where should we reinvest the time AI frees up to build depth, taste, and ownership that competitors can't easily copy."

## Best Newsletter Candidate
**Move slow and make things: What Japanese craftsmanship can teach business about quality, patience, and expertise when AI makes speed cheap** (Fortune) has the deepest org psych angle and the richest material to work with since it's based on full article text. It gives Pete four distinct, well-developed frameworks—going deep not wide (Shimano), taste as a cultivated skill (Japanese denim makers), patience as strategy (YKK's vertical integration), and signing your work (the Avedis cymbal story)—each of which can anchor its own section in a longer piece. This is rich enough to connect directly to Pete's core thesis: that organizational identity, expertise development, and long-term trust-building are strategic assets, not soft extras, and that leaders need to be deliberate about where they redirect capacity once AI absorbs routine work. A 500+ word newsletter could walk through each principle and translate it into a concrete HR/COO action—like redesigning career paths around depth rather than breadth, or building vertical ownership of key processes as a retention and quality strategy.
