# Morning Briefing — November 15, 2026

## This Week's Themes

The dominant theme this week is founder-centric power and the governance failures it enables—seen across the entire Nvidia saga (**NVIDIA Announces CEO Transition** (Hacker News), **Jensen Huang is the AI boom's key man. Now, that's a problem** (Fortune)) and echoed in **Eric Trump-backed robot soldier startup can't pay staff after founder borrowed $4.6 million from company to buy home** (CNBC Business), where concentrated, unchecked authority leads directly to organizational harm. A closely related thread is conflict-of-interest blindness: **Where was Nvidia's security team?** (TechCrunch) and **Suspected China spy linked to Nvidia's ex-boss left in 2025, Megaspeed says** (BBC Business) both show how personal relationships and siloed oversight structures let national-security-level risks go unflagged. A third theme is AI adoption and the credibility gap between leaders and employees, spanning **I helped grow a steel company from $750 million to $12 billion. CEOs should use AI before they grade employees on it** (Fortune), **Gen Z believes using AI is making their colleagues dumb and lazy—but may paradoxically see it as key to their own promotion, Wharton says** (Fortune), and **Databricks' new AI agent tells CEO Ali Ghodsi the unvarnished truth** (TechCrunch). Finally, change management under pressure surfaces in **Transportation secretary blames 'routine' equipment upgrade for grounding flights nationwide** (TechCrunch) and **Inside Dan Schulman's war on the old Verizon** (Fortune), both examining how leaders respond—well or badly—to operational breakdowns.

## Top Story

**Where was Nvidia's security team?** (TechCrunch) is the most consequential story today because it names a structural failure CHROs can't ignore: security teams scoped narrowly to cyber/physical threats, while HR and legal own executive conduct, with no bridge between them. That silo let a conflict-of-interest with national security implications go unaddressed until it became a crisis. It's a direct, actionable warning for how org design—not individual judgment—creates blind spots.

## Best LinkedIn Post Candidate

**I helped grow a steel company from $750 million to $12 billion. CEOs should use AI before they grade employees on it** (Fortune) works because it's a personal, credible narrative (not abstract research) that exposes a leadership hypocrisy Pete's audience will recognize instantly.
Suggested hook: "If you're grading your team on AI use but haven't used it yourself to make a single strategic decision, you don't have an AI policy—you have a trust problem."

## Best Newsletter Candidate

**Gen Z believes using AI is making their colleagues dumb and lazy—but may paradoxically see it as key to their own promotion, Wharton says** (Fortune) has the deepest org psych substance here—it's built on a nearly 2,500-person Wharton survey and names two distinct, well-established cognitive biases (present bias and the better-than-average effect) driving a generational paradox. It gives Pete room to go deep: explain the biases, connect them to the "crap vs. craft" framework, and translate it into a concrete CHRO action—AI governance by task type instead of blanket policy—plus the looming talent-pipeline risk of eroding critical thinking. That's a 500+ word piece with research, tension, and a clear prescriptive takeaway.

## Best Workshop/Keynote Example

**Transportation secretary blames 'routine' equipment upgrade for grounding flights nationwide** (TechCrunch) is a strong live-case illustration of psychological safety and blame culture: a contractor pushes a critical infrastructure change during peak traffic with no union consultation, and the public leadership response speculates about "incompetence" before root cause is known. It's a clean, high-stakes example to use when teaching why blame-first responses shut down transparent error reporting.
