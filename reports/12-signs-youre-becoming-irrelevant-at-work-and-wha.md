# 12 signs you’re becoming irrelevant at work (and what to do about it)

**Source:** Fast Company  
**Published:** 2026-09-09T10:07:00  
**URL:** [https://www.fastcompany.com/91588217/12-signs-youre-becoming-irrelevant-at-work-and-how-to-fix-it-irrelevant-work-career-advice](https://www.fastcompany.com/91588217/12-signs-youre-becoming-irrelevant-at-work-and-how-to-fix-it-irrelevant-work-career-advice)

## Summary
The article identifies 12 signs of becoming irrelevant at work: people stop bringing you problems, you stop asking questions, you default to 'how' before 'why,' you're valued only for execution not judgment, you can't link your work to business outcomes, AI can replicate your tasks, your name stops coming up for opportunities, junior employees outpace you, you rely on past expertise instead of learning, you focus on procedures over strategy, your sales decline without obvious cause, and you no longer recognize industry developments. Practical remedies include owning unclaimed problems, cultivating curiosity, conducting personal impact audits, mastering AI tools, architecting your reputation, seeking reverse mentoring from junior peers, and pursuing quarterly stretch projects.

## Organizational Psychology Angle
The article touches on professional obsolescence as a socially-constructed phenomenon — relevance erodes through relational and perceptual shifts (being excluded from decisions, losing informal influence) before technical competence declines, aligning with research on job crafting, role identity, and social capital in organizations.

## Leadership Lesson
CHROs and COOs should build systems that signal relevance erosion early — such as tracking inclusion in cross-functional decisions, encouraging reverse mentoring, and creating quarterly stretch assignments — rather than waiting for performance reviews to surface disengagement or skill stagnation.

## Original Story Summary
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Feeling overlooked in meetings or left out of key decisions can signal a troubling shift in your workplace standing. This article outlines 12 specific warning signs that your professional relevance may be slipping, along with practical strategies to reverse the trend. Here&#8217;s how to rebuild your impact, from leveraging <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/artificial-intelligence" title="AI">AI</a> tools to repositioning yourself as a strategic thinker rather than just an executor.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-own-an-unclaimed-problem-publicly">Own An Unclaimed Problem Publicly</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The clearest sign a professional is becoming irrelevant is that people stop bringing them problems. Most professionals watch their skills for signs of decline, but relevance fails socially before it fails technically.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">When colleagues route around you, and you are informed of decisions instead of being consulted before them, the slide has already started. The course correction is to go get a live problem. Pick one unowned issue the business is feeling right now, take visible ownership of it, and share what you learn in plain language as you go.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">At one client company, an operations manager had been reduced to giving status updates. He took over a chronic fulfillment backlog that nobody owned, fixed it step by step, and documented the process for the whole team. Within a quarter, leadership was pulling him back into decisions. Relevance is rebuilt by owning problems, not by defending a title.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/kamyarshah">Kamyar Shah</a>, fractional COO, <a href="https://www.worldconsultinggroup.com">World Consulting Group</a></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-ask-more-questions-for-one-week">Ask More Questions For One Week</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Another sign a professional is becoming irrelevant is that they&#8217;ve stopped asking questions and started only answering them.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I&#8217;ve watched it happen in boardrooms from Dallas to Seoul. The professional who used to challenge assumptions, push back on conventional thinking, and ask, &#8220;Why are we doing it this way?&#8221; becomes the person who just executes. They&#8217;re still competent. They still show up. But they&#8217;ve stopped being curious, and that&#8217;s when relevance starts to erode.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I didn&#8217;t just observe this from the outside. I lived it. When I was running a tutoring company in Los Angeles, I made myself so indispensable, so central to every answer, that I stopped asking the questions that would have helped the business grow. I had confused being needed with being effective. The company dissolved. What survived was the lesson: The moment you stop being curious about your own work, you&#8217;ve already started falling behind.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">After two decades of coaching Fortune 500 executives and teaching at the University of Southern California, I&#8217;ve come to believe that curiosity is not a personality trait. It&#8217;s a professional discipline. And like any discipline, it can be practiced or neglected.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The one action I give every professional who feels their relevance slipping: Spend one week asking more questions than you answer. In meetings, on calls, in your own head before you give the standard response. Not performative questions. Genuine ones. What don&#8217;t I understand here? What am I assuming? What would I do differently if I were new to this?</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The leaders I&#8217;ve seen successfully course-correct all have one thing in common. They got curious again before they got desperate.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Curiosity isn&#8217;t a distraction. It&#8217;s direction. And in my experience, it&#8217;s the most reliable early-warning system a professional has.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/iamjustinkey">Justin D. Key</a>, actor, author &amp; executive coach, <a href="https://www.blacktheorem.com">Black Theorem Co.</a></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-start-with-why-not-how">Start With Why, Not How</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">What does it mean to be irrelevant?</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">According to Merriam-Webster, it means &#8220;having no significant bearing on the matter at hand.&#8221;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Here&#8217;s the uncomfortable truth: Becoming irrelevant rarely happens because someone stops working hard. It happens because they keep offering answers that no longer have a significant bearing on the business&#8217;s most important challenges.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I&#8217;ve spent years helping organizations make smarter investments in leadership development, and I keep coming back to one core idea: Start with the problem, not the oh-so-seductive solution. That mindset shift has never been more important.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">One sign you&#8217;re becoming irrelevant is when your first instinct is to recommend a &#8220;how&#8221; before you&#8217;ve fully understood, or even become curious about, the &#8220;why.&#8221;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For example, a business leader says, &#8220;We need a communication workshop.&#8221; A talent investor on the path to irrelevance will jump straight to the solution and immediately start thinking about facilitators, activities, and whether to make it a half-day or full-day session.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A strategic talent investor will course-correct and ask, &#8220;Help me understand your underlying why. What&#8217;s happening in the business that&#8217;s leading you to request communication training?&#8221;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Maybe customer complaints are increasing because departments aren&#8217;t coordinating well. Maybe managers aren&#8217;t cascading priorities. Or maybe communication isn&#8217;t the problem at all.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">When you shift the conversation from, &#8220;What training do you want?&#8221; to &#8220;What business outcome are we trying to achieve?&#8221; everything changes. You&#8217;re no longer filling requests and executing blindly. You&#8217;re helping leaders solve business problems.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That&#8217;s how you stay relevant.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/tinarobinsonspeaks">Tina Robinson</a>, founder and CEO, <a href="https://www.workjoycoaching.com">WorkJoy</a></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-share-your-perspective-beyond-delivery">Share Your Perspective Beyond Delivery</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Another giveaway that a professional is becoming irrelevant is when they stop being sought out for their judgment and are used only for their execution.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Early in a career, being the person who gets things done is a strength. Over time, if that is still the primary reason people come to you, something has stalled. The professionals who remain relevant as industries evolve are the ones whose thinking is valued, not just their output. When colleagues and clients stop asking what you think and start only asking what you can deliver, that is the signal worth paying attention to.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The specific action that course-corrects it is deceptively simple: Start sharing your point of view publicly and consistently, in a format that reaches beyond your immediate circle.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The example I can speak to directly is LinkedIn. I publish daily, and the discipline of forming and articulating a genuine perspective on the problems my clients and industry are navigating has done more for my professional relevance than any credential or accomplishment. Not because the platform itself is magic, but because the act of developing a clear point of view, defending it, and refining it through public conversation forces a kind of intellectual sharpening that execution-only work never requires.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Professionals who are becoming invisible are usually invisible to themselves first. They are busy, delivering, meeting deadlines, and not noticing that the nature of their contribution has narrowed. The antidote is not working harder at what they already do. It is developing and demonstrating a perspective that only their specific combination of experience and insight could produce. That is what makes someone genuinely difficult to replace: not the tasks they complete, but the thinking they bring that nobody else brings quite the same way.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/derekfredrickson">Derek Fredrickson</a>, founder &amp; CEO, <a href="https://thecoosolution.com">The COO Solution</a></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-audit-impact-and-align-work-to-outcomes">Audit Impact And Align Work To Outcomes</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">One of the most obvious signs that a professional is no longer relevant is when they cannot trace the work that they do each day to growth in the organization&#8217;s financial or operational performance. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For example, when they are asked about their achievements, they will refer to their process (e.g., how many meetings held, emails sent, and reports produced) as opposed to the result related to the performance of the business (e.g., revenue produced, cost savings, or time savings). Their focus becomes protecting their internal routine and, as a result, they will stop noticing changes in the market around them.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">To correct the course, professionals need to be conducting a monthly personal impact audit on themselves and reviewing their tasks, and making sure that each task is connected directly to a core company goal. Tasks that cannot be linked to benefits related to reducing costs, generating revenues, or reducing risk should be flagged for elimination or redesign.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I have seen this disconnect first hand when budgets are being cut. As an example, I had a <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/marketing" title="Marketing">marketing</a> manager who quantified their monthly workload as &#8220;20 new creative assets launched.&#8221; This purely relates to their process. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Another manager tracked the performance of those marketing assets and identified three outdated formats that were causing the marketing budget to bleed; as a result, they stopped using those formats, which reduced their customer acquisition cost by 12%. In this instance, rather than tracking process outputs, the manager positively impacted marketing efficiency.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/tetiana-hnatiuk-46355a164">Tetiana Hnatiuk</a>, СОО, <a href="https://skylum.com">Skylum</a></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-master-automation-before-someone-else-does">Master Automation Before Someone Else Does</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Another sign is when someone keeps talking about what they did instead of what problem they can solve today.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Industries change too fast for experience alone to remain valuable. If your expertise depends on doing a task that AI can now complete in minutes, your value has shifted.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">It now lies in your judgment, your ability to ask better questions, and your understanding of the business rather than what you have done in the past alone.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The fastest way to address that is to pick one part of your job that AI can already do well and learn to use it before someone else does. Don&#8217;t compete with the tool. Become the person who knows when to trust it, when to challenge it, and how to turn its output into business outcomes.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We&#8217;ve seen recruiters who once spent hours screening résumés now use AI for the first pass. The recruiters who stand out aren&#8217;t the ones reviewing more CVs. They&#8217;re the ones spending the time they saved wisely: building stronger candidate relationships and helping <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/hiring" title="Hiring">hiring</a> managers make better decisions. That&#8217;s where the value has moved.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">As Nvidia&#8217;s founder, Jensen Huang, famously put it: “The person who uses AI is going to take your job.” I would lean into that and say, be that smart person.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/adithyan">Adithyan RK</a>, CEO, <a href="https://hyring.com">Hyring</a></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-architect-a-reputation-others-recall">Architect A Reputation Others Recall</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">One of the clearest signs is when the professional stops being talked about.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">It seems obvious, but let me explain. If your name never comes up when an opportunity arises, or if your expertise isn&#8217;t visible beyond your immediate workplace, then you&#8217;re at risk of becoming invisible and irrelevant. And in today&#8217;s market, with AI tools leveling the playing field, invisibility is a much greater threat than incompetence.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The most effective way to course-correct is to begin architecting your reputation rather than leaving it to chance. When someone searches your name on Google or AI, you want that narrative to align with what you want and to be in your favor.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Reputation architecture means to deliberately shape what you&#8217;re known for through your leadership, communication, and digital presence. It shifts your reputation from simply doing excellent work to making sure your expertise is remembered and trusted by the people who matter.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We recently worked with a Sydney-based CEO who had built an outstanding corporate career and now was looking to transition into advisory and board work. She had the experience and capability, but outside her organization very few people understood the value she could bring. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We stepped in and built a strategy that reflected her leadership philosophy, helped her effectively share her expertise publicly, and repositioned her for the right audience. This was how she created market memory. Her capabilities didn&#8217;t change, but now her reputation finally reflected it to the right people. A professional&#8217;s reputation is the biggest opportunity maker and key differentiator that separates you from the person sitting across you.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">With the rollout of AI, expertise and skills are becoming easier to copy. Just think about the tech companies. However, reputation isn&#8217;t. The professionals who will remain relevant won&#8217;t necessarily be those who know the most but rather those who have authority and are trusted for the value they bring.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/patricia-bosa-personal-branding-director">Patricia Bosa</a>, personal <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/branding" title="Branding">branding</a> director, <a href="https://imagegroup.com.au">Image Group International</a></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-let-faster-peers-coach-you">Let Faster Peers Coach You</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">When a junior employee or an early-career professional who recently joined your team starts to outpace you, completing tasks in a couple of hours that usually take you a day to complete, then your role is vulnerable.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">What many professionals fall victim to is the &#8220;if it ain&#8217;t broke, don&#8217;t fix it&#8221; mentality. Building their entire value proposition around, &#8220;I put in the hours,&#8221; simply does not cut it in today&#8217;s fast-paced business environment. If you are in the position where your speed, scale, and technical agility are outmatched by a junior employee, you will have to course-correct.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Let&#8217;s say you are a traditional marketing content manager. You excel at SEO practices and copywriting. The landscape has changed, and search engines are not looking for keyword-stuffed blog content; they want purpose, there has to be intent, and you have to show up in AI overviews, because standard blogging is no longer moving the traffic needle. The manager saw AI overviews only as a fad, something superficial that wouldn&#8217;t last, and because of this, the entire marketing department&#8217;s ROI started a downward trend. Instead of giving him the chance to course-correct, the company decides to replace him with a new digital growth manager.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Before this happens, put your professional ego away and ask the younger, more agile team members to show you how they use tech to drive results, or how they set up their workflows. Use this fresh perspective with your indispensable knowledge to upskill yourself and to remain relevant within your role.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/shawnmichaelhill">Shawn Hill</a>, VP of growth, <a href="https://www.movebuddha.com">MoveBuddha</a></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-pursue-quarterly-stretch-projects">Pursue Quarterly Stretch Projects</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Another indicator of irrelevance is when someone continues to rely on past expertise instead of staying curious about what&#8217;s changing around them.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In today&#8217;s workplace, technical skills can become outdated surprisingly quickly. What remains valuable is the ability to learn, adapt, and contribute in new ways. Professionals who believe that &#8220;this is how we&#8217;ve always done it&#8221; often find themselves left behind—not because they lack capability, but because they stop evolving.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We&#8217;ve interacted with hundreds of organizations and people leaders. One pattern stands out consistently: The professionals who continue to grow are those who seek feedback, volunteer for unfamiliar projects, and make learning part of their routine rather than something they do only when required.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A simple but powerful course correction is to deliberately step outside your comfort zone every quarter. Take ownership of a cross-functional initiative, learn an emerging technology, or develop a business skill beyond your current role. Relevance is built through continuous adaptation, not tenure.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I remember a discussion during one of our HR leadership roundtables where a chief HR officer shared the story of a long-serving professional who had built an excellent career in traditional HR operations. As the organization embraced AI-enabled talent practices and data-driven decision-making, she realized her existing expertise alone would no longer be enough. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Instead of resisting the change, she enrolled in analytics and AI courses, partnered with the HR technology team on a pilot project, and gradually became the bridge between technology and people practices. Within a year, she wasn&#8217;t just keeping pace—she had become one of the organization&#8217;s most trusted voices on the future of HR.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The lesson is simple: Relevance is rarely lost overnight. It fades when learning stops. The professionals who remain indispensable are not necessarily those who know the most today, but those who are committed to learning what tomorrow demands.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/ekta-capoor">Ekta C</a>apoor, director, cofounder &amp; editor-in-chief, <a href="https://amazingworkplaces.co">Amazing Workplaces</a></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-shift-from-execution-to-strategy">Shift From Execution To Strategy</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">One way to tell if a professional is irrelevant is by watching them switch their focus from achieving positive business results to simply executing on standard operating procedures. For example, with software development, if someone is performing at a high level in terms of writing functional code but does not understand how that code affects a product&#8217;s performance or customer retention, they are on the verge of losing their job due to AI technologies automating their current level of work.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">To reverse becoming irrelevant, a professional must move from being an execution-level contributor to being an architectural-level or strategic-level thinker. A common way to begin this process is by shadowing the product or business development team of their company in order to gain a deeper understanding of the product, its unit economics, what causes customers to leave (or to stay), and how specific features of the product help generate revenue for the company.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">An example of this transformation occurred recently when a senior QA engineer had his job threatened by automated testing systems and processes. Rather than looking for another automated testing framework, the engineer decided to master how to analyze customer data and collaborate with other departments to identify areas where potential bottlenecks could cause harm before any code is ever written. Through this process, the engineer transitioned from being a typical quality assurance tester to being a key component of the organization&#8217;s risk management team.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/taras-tymoshchuk-3071243b">Taras Tymoshchuk</a>, CEO, cofounder, <a href="https://geniusee.com">Geniusee</a></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-partner-with-competitors-to-spot-gaps">Partner With Competitors To Spot Gaps</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">An unmistakable sign of becoming irrelevant is simply a drop in sales. The market responds quickly to irrelevance, and if numbers are declining without an obvious cause, that is often the reason. Customers are often quicker to notice a shift than the professionals themselves. To address the problem, build friendlier relationships with your competitors. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Rather than treating competitors as threats, share and exchange ideas with them. This will reveal blind spots while expanding relationships. In my experience, there was a period where referrals slowed, and it was not immediately clear to me why. Talking with competing firms made it clear that clients were increasingly asking for broader, more holistic services that we had not yet built out. That conversation led directly to expanding our service offerings and refining our marketing approach.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/jackmperkins">Jack Perkins</a>, founder &amp; managing director, <a href="https://adaptivecp.com">Adaptive Capital Partners</a></p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-track-industry-briefs-daily-and-deepen-monthly">Track Industry Briefs Daily And Deepen Monthly</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Here&#8217;s one you&#8217;d recognize in yourself: It&#8217;s when you no longer recognize the headlines or developments that others are sharing. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">You don&#8217;t need intimate knowledge of every strategy, tactic, or piece of news in your industry, but you should have a broad and fairly deep command of most updates. If you find yourself increasingly confused, overwhelmed, and left out of what others are building, sharing and discussing, that&#8217;s a clear sign. It won&#8217;t happen suddenly. It usually creeps in over months or years.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">One action you can take is to find the top five newsletters in your industry and spend 10-15 minutes each morning reading the latest developments. From there, choose one or two of those concepts a month to learn more about, dive deeper into or integrate into your process.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/kenmagma">Ken Marshall</a>, cofounder, <a href="https://meetsona.ai">Meet Sona</a></p>
