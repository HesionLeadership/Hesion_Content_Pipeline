# Why ‘don’t come to me with problems—come to me with solutions’ is bad advice

**Source:** Fast Company  
**Published:** 2026-08-25T08:00:00  
**URL:** [https://www.fastcompany.com/91593090/dont-come-to-me-with-problems-come-to-me-with-solutions-bad-advice](https://www.fastcompany.com/91593090/dont-come-to-me-with-problems-come-to-me-with-solutions-bad-advice)

## Summary
The common managerial admonition 'don't come to me with problems—come to me with solutions' inadvertently silences employees, delays early warning signs, and misunderstands how problem-solving works in complex organizations. Leaders should instead invite early problem-raising and collaborative problem-solving, saying things like 'Bring problems early, so we can approach them productively' or 'You don't have to have the solution, but come ready to think with me.'

## Organizational Psychology Angle
This directly addresses psychological safety and employee voice—research consistently shows that when employees perceive problem-raising as risky or unwelcome, they self-censor, leading to preventable failures and organizational silence. The article highlights how leadership communication norms shape whether organizations detect threats early or sleepwalk into crises.

## Leadership Lesson
CHROs and COOs should audit the implicit messages their leadership norms send about problem-raising. Replace 'solutions-only' expectations with structured norms that reward early escalation, clear problem-framing, and collaborative sensemaking—and critically, ensure leaders respond to raised problems with curiosity rather than blame, or the new language will ring hollow.

## Original Story Summary
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">“Don’t come to me with problems. Come to me with solutions.”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">You’ve almost certainly heard this line. You may have even said it yourself. It sounds both tough-minded and empowering. But of all the things managers say with good intentions, this is my least favorite. It inadvertently sends two unhelpful messages: First, that problems aren’t welcome, and second, that solving them is something you should do on your own. Both are exactly wrong in today’s complex, uncertain world.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">As a management aphorism, it’s in direct tension with what the best leaders tend to say and do. Colin Powell once said: “Leadership is solving problems. The day soldiers stop bringing you their problems is the day you have stopped leading them. They have either lost confidence that you can help or concluded that you do not care. Either case is a failure of leadership.”&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Having spent much of my research career studying why people fail to speak up with their concerns, questions, and even ideas at work, Powell’s words seem refreshingly wise to me. And they remind me of something Fujio Cho, then the Toyota plant manager in Georgetown, Kentucky (and later chairman of Toyota worldwide) said in a senior leadership meeting to a subordinate named Jim Wiseman, as reported in <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/58345/no-satisfaction-toyota" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank"><em>Fast Company</em></a> years ago: “Please talk to us about your problems so we can work on them together.”</p>





<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-unpacking-impact"><br />Unpacking impact</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Let’s start with what “don’t come to me with problems—come to me with solutions” actually conveys to subordinates. Although its <em>intent</em> may be to ask people to take initiative, to think critically about options rather than just dropping problems in the boss’s lap, or to discourage passive complaining, its <em>impact</em> is something else entirely. It is not brilliant solutions. It’s silence. It’s rework. Ultimately, it gives rise to, “How did this get so bad without anyone telling me?”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">When people are told, “Don’t come to me with problems,” they hear, “Problems make you look weak, negative, or difficult.” They hear, “I don’t want to hear bad news.” Employees—especially those early in their careers or far from the center of power—are exquisitely <a href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228017697_Managing_the_Risk_of_Learning_Psychological_Safety_in_Work_Teams" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">attuned</a> to what might get them labeled as a poor performer, or worse, a troublemaker. As a result, they <a href="https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=54851" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">naturally hold back</a> half-formed concerns. They delay sharing early warning signs until the problem has grown so large that it can no longer be hidden.   </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">To be fair, there might have been a time and a context where this admonition made sense. In contexts characterized by relatively high levels of consistency and predictability, and especially when interdependence and complexity were relatively low, problem-detecting and -solving often paired together neatly. Expert work that’s neatly siloed—think of a brilliant diagnostician in a narrow field of medical expertise—also allows this to occur.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We see versions of this pattern in postmortems of crises and major corporate failures. In inquiry after inquiry—from <a href="https://hbr.org/2006/11/facing-ambiguous-threats" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">NASA shuttle failures</a> to VW “<a href="https://www.reuters.com/article/us-volkswagen-emissions-culture/fear-and-respect-vws-culture-under-winterkorn-idUSKCN0S40MT20151010/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Dieselgate</a>” and <a href="https://store.hbr.org/product/theranos-the-unicorn-that-wasn-t/319068?sku=319068-PDF-ENG" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Theranos</a>—investigators discovered that warning signs were present long before failures hit the headlines. Employees may have raised safety concerns, questioned aggressive targets, or flagged vulnerabilities—only to be ignored or discouraged. Months before Equifax’s massive data breach, for example, a security researcher <a href="https://www.wired.com/story/equifax-warned-of-vulnerability-months-before-breach/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">warned </a>the company that it was vulnerable to a large-scale attack.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Research across industries finds that people routinely stay silent about problems because they fear retaliation, doubt anything will change, or simply don’t believe their concerns are welcome. A crisp aphorism about not bringing problems to the boss only reinforces that reluctance. It tells people, in so many words, that their job is to cope, not to name the underlying issue.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-problem-solving-in-today-s-organizations">Problem-solving in today’s organizations</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The second issue with “Don’t come to me with problems” is just as important but less obvious: It misunderstands how problem-solving actually works in modern organizations. Spotting problems is something individuals are good at. The customer service representative hears the same complaint five times in a week. A nurse notices that a piece of equipment is confusing and easy to misuse. A junior engineer keeps tripping over the same bottleneck in the code review process. We are excellent problem-spotters, especially when we’re close to where the work happens.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">But individuals rarely have all the information, resources, or authority required to design and implement a solution on their own. In real organizations, consequential problems almost always cut across functional, geographic, or hierarchical lines. Fixing a recurring customer issue might involve product and operations, legal and finance, headquarters and the field. Improving a safety process might require coordination between frontline staff, supervisors, training, and compliance.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In this context, “Come to me with solutions” lands as: “Unless you can single-handedly architect the cross-functional remedy, keep quiet.” It tells the people best positioned to notice emerging issues that their observations are incomplete and therefore unwelcome. That’s how organizations sleepwalk into <a href="https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Right-Kind-of-Wrong/Amy-C-Edmondson/9781982195069" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">preventable failures</a>. The lone-hero model of problem-solving is a poor fit for complex, interdependent systems. It encourages a subtler form of waste as well: Teams invest energy in <a href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2307/41166165" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">work-arounds</a> that not only don’t solve the problem, but also give rise to dynamics that worsen the problem over time.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">High-performing teams are not defined by the absence of problems; they’re defined by the speed and candor with which problems surface. The faster people in an organization can make sense of a shared reality, the faster the organization can respond.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-an-alternative">An alternative</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">What should leaders say instead? I recognize that the phrase captures a legitimate sentiment—a desire for initiative instead of passive complaining. The question is how to cultivate that without shutting down the flow of vital information.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">One option is: “Bring problems early, so we can approach them productively.” This shifts the emphasis from expecting ready-made solutions to the important work of understanding the problem as clearly as possible so as to develop possible solutions. Good questions in this conversation might include: What are you seeing? How often? What examples or data points do you have? What makes this concerning? Framing a problem clearly is itself a contribution. The goal is to bring the right people together to ask good questions and generate options.&nbsp;&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Another might be: “You don’t have to have the solution, but come ready to think with me.” This preserves the expectation of engagement and ownership. It’s not permission to dump vague discomfort on someone else’s desk but rather an invitation to collaborate.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Relatedly, a powerful reframing is: “Escalating a problem is an act of leadership, not disloyalty.” In many organizations, people worry that surfacing problems will make their team, their unit, or their boss look bad. They stay quiet out of a misguided sense of loyalty. As Netflix cofounder Reed Hastings wrote in his <a href="https://www.google.com/books/edition/No_Rules_Rules/XaZAzQEACAAJ?hl=en" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">book, <em>No Rules Rules</em></a>: “It is tantamount to being disloyal to the company if you fail to speak up when you disagree with a colleague or have feedback that could be helpful. After all, you could help the business—but you are choosing not to.” </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The same is true for problems. Explicitly reframing problem-raising as a contribution to the collective helps create <a href="https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=67307" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">psychological safety</a> for speaking up. When people know they can raise concerns without having everything figured out, and that they will be expected to participate in the thinking, they’re far more likely to speak up early and energetically. The contrasting cultures that leaders’ approaches to problem-finding create are captured in the table below.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-going-forward">Going forward</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Words alone are not enough, of course. Leadership is more than simply swapping out slogans. If people bring messy problems forward early and are met with irritation or blame, they will learn the lesson quickly. To make these norms real, leaders must respond to problem-raising with curiosity, appreciation, and a genuine invitation to think together. That is how you teach people that <em>their</em> problems are, in Gen. Powell’s sense, <em>your</em> work.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The impulse behind “Don’t come to me with problems” is understandable. Most leaders don’t want to become complaint departments. But if you want a resilient, innovative organization, you need people to be expert problem-spotters and collaborative problem-solvers. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">It’s time to retire that well-worn aphorism. Take Powell’s reminder seriously—when people stop bringing you problems, you have stopped leading them. Saying “Bring me problems early and be ready to think together” is less catchy—but far more effective.</p>
