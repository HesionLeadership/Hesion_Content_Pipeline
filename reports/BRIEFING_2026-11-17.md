# Morning Briefing — November 17, 2026

## This Week's Themes

The dominant theme this week is founder-centric governance failure, driven by the unraveling Nvidia scandal across more than a dozen stories including **Jensen Huang says he would be 'honored' if allowed to return as Nvidia CEO** (TechCrunch), **Jensen Huang Says He Would Return to Nvidia if Asked** (NYT Business RSS), **US government confirms investigation into tech firm Megaspeed** (BBC Business), and **Nvidia's new chairman Harvey Jones is a 'charming elder statesman' set to lead the search for the next CEO. But did he enable the current crisis?** (Fortune) — all pointing to the dangers of centralized authority without succession planning or conflict-of-interest guardrails. A parallel theme is succession planning done right, contrasted sharply by **Home Depot names Richard McPhail as new CEO, beats earnings expectations** (CNBC Business) and Nvidia's reliance on **Colette Kress, the CFO who challenged Huang's bets, is Nvidia's interim CEO** (Reuters CEO via Google). A third theme is AI's reshaping of leadership trust, seen in **Honeywell CEO says AI isn't about replacing workers as C.H. Robinson touts 60% productivity gains: 'We all have to reskill ourselves'** (Fortune) and **I helped grow a steel company from $750 million to $12 billion. CEOs should use AI before they grade employees on it** (Fortune), both emphasizing leaders modeling behavior over mandating it. Finally, a smaller thread on leadership psychology assessment emerges through **Who Will Make a Great Leader? There's a Test for That** (HBR) and **Bots in the boardroom: Could an AI ever replace board directors?** (Fortune), both questioning traditional signals of leadership fitness.

## Top Story

**Who Will Make a Great Leader? There's a Test for That** (HBR) is the standout because it's grounded in actual research, not anecdote — Deming's finding that people who most want to be managers perform worse than randomly assigned ones is a direct challenge to how most organizations handle promotion decisions. For CHROs building succession pipelines, this is ammunition to rethink self-nomination-based promotion criteria entirely.

Suggested hook: "10,000 employees want their disgraced CEO back. Here's why that should worry every board member."

## Best Newsletter Candidate

**Jensen Huang Says He Would Return to Nvidia if Asked** (NYT Business RSS) has the deepest org psych terrain to mine for 500+ words. It's not just a scandal recap — it's a case study in founder-CEO indispensability colliding with governance accountability in real time. Pete could unpack: why boards freeze under public/investor pressure campaigns rather than following pre-set misconduct protocols; how "employee and investor support" gets weaponized as a legitimacy argument even when the underlying conduct (affairs with a direct report, ties to a security-flagged individual) is undisputed; and the power-distance dynamics baked into a CEO-subordinate relationship that make "support" itself suspect. There's also a secondary thread worth exploring — the diffusion of accountability, since the piece notes Huang cites support from employees, investors, and partners as justification, effectively outsourcing the ethics question to stakeholder sentiment rather than board judgment. This is exactly the kind of story CHROs forward to each other: a real, unresolved governance failure they could be facing themselves. A newsletter could end with a practical framework — pre-defined misconduct thresholds, clear escalation protocols, and succession benches — that boards should build now, before their own "bring the CEO back" campaign starts trending.

## Best Workshop/Keynote Example

**Home Depot names Richard McPhail as new CEO, beats earnings expectations** (CNBC Business) is a clean, positive counter-example to use alongside the Nvidia chaos. It illustrates textbook planned succession: internal promotion, a graceful exit into a board chair role for the outgoing CEO, and simultaneous executive reshuffling to signal stability. Great for contrasting "succession done right" vs. "succession done in crisis."
