<hr class="wp-block-separator has-alpha-channel-opacity" />



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Nitin Nohria has studied and educated CEOs for three decades. Since 1997, the former dean of Harvard Business School, now executive chairman of Thrive Capital, has taught the university’s <a href="https://www.exed.hbs.edu/chief-executive-leadership-programs/new-ceo-workshop" rel="noopener" target="_blank">New CEO Workshop</a>, an invitation-only program offered through its executive education arm. In 2018, he and the economist Michael E. Porter wrote a groundbreaking study on how CEOs spend their time. And last month he published a new book titled <a href="https://bookshop.org/p/books/the-ceo-the-role-the-reality-the-responsibility-nitin-nohria/d0c4097851abff5e" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><em>The CEO: The Role, the Reality, the Responsibility</em>.</a>  </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Given this body of work, I was eager to speak with Nohria about how the CEO role has changed over the past 30 years, especially when so many executives feel the job has become more complex and demanding. Nohria says the <em>context</em> in which CEOs lead changes all the time—technology, social mores, regulations all evolve—but the <em>role</em> remains largely unchanged.  </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">“The role of the CEO is surprisingly enduring,” he says. “It’s still to craft a vision for how the company will navigate the moment. It is to select an extraordinary senior team, [and] it is to align the organization [to] allow everybody else to make decisions that you can celebrate.”&nbsp;</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-same-role-different-demands">Same role, different demands</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">There’s also a consistency to the experience of becoming a CEO. Nohria says for 30 years he’s been asking newbies to identify the things that surprised them upon taking the job, and the same answers rise to the top: They’re unprepared to always be in the spotlight; they quickly come to the realization that they cannot run the company by making every decision themselves; and their schedules, already packed and demanding, become even more fragmented. <br /><br />Nohria says they need to remind themselves: “I can’t outwork this job. And my job is to shape the context where others can make decisions that I celebrate, as opposed to being the person who feels like they are driving the performance of this company.” </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Nohria sees small differences in the way chief executives spend their time today. Since the COVID-19 pandemic, virtual meetings are more commonplace today than they were when his research about how CEOs spend their time was first published. Chief executives may find themselves engaging with regulators more than they did a decade ago.  </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Still, other stakeholders, such as customers and frontline employees, continue to get short shrift. (Nohria and Porter’s research, which entailed coding CEOs’ time in 15-minute increments, 24 hours a day, found that CEOs spent just 3% of time with customers.) “There are so many demands on a CEO’s time that the squeaky wheel gets the grease, and these are the two [constituencies] that don’t always make demands,” Noria observes. His advice for CEOs who want to make customer and employee visits a priority: “Put it on your calendar. If it isn’t scheduled, the risk that it’s going to get lost is very high in a CEO’s life.”&nbsp;&nbsp;</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-efficiency-and-an-emanuel-shout-out">Efficiency—and an Emanuel shout-out</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Nohria says <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/artificial-intelligence" title="AI">artificial intelligence</a> tools are just starting to have an impact on the ways CEOs manage their time, with some using tools such as Copilot to help them analyze their calendars, perhaps reminding them to consider how they’re allocating their time. For example, an AI assistant could encourage moving a few things around to allow for more meetings with employees and customers. “Almost like a little bit of a good chief of staff that’s nudging you to do the right thing,” he notes. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Nohria makes a somewhat unexpected appearance in <em>Roll the Calls,</em> the autobiography of the Hollywood agent Ari Emanuel. Nohria once spent a day and a half advising Emanuel as he contemplated merging his Endeavor talent agency with another firm. While I surmised that the combustible and argumentative Emanuel is unlike most of the corporate CEOs who participate in HBS’s executive education programs, Nohria says, “He has a constancy and a driven purpose about getting things done, and I’ve always admired that about him.” As for Nohria’s observations on how Emanuel manages his time? “I haven’t studied his calendar, but if I had to guess, I think Ari would probably have more meetings in a single day than almost anybody I’ve studied.” </p>
