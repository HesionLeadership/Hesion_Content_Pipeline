# I coach world No. 1 tennis player Aryna Sabalenka. Here’s what productivity culture gets completely wrong about pressure

## Summary
Tennis coach Jason Stacy, who coaches world No. 1 Aryna Sabalenka, argues that productivity culture's focus on habits and hacks ('apps') fails under pressure because it neglects the deeper 'operating system'—energy, emotions, and environment. He distinguishes between 'Fighters' (reactive, adrenaline-driven, prone to burnout) and 'Warriors' (prepared, emotionally regulated, sustainable), arguing that true performance under pressure comes from self-trust and internal alignment, not optimized hustle.

## Organizational Psychology Angle
The Fighter vs. Warrior framework maps directly onto research on self-regulation depletion and sustainable high performance—organizations that reward constant urgency ('fighter mode') systematically erode the psychological resources leaders need when stakes are highest. The emphasis on self-trust as a precondition for freedom under pressure aligns with self-efficacy theory and research on ego depletion under stress.

## Leadership Lesson
CHROs and COOs should recognize that investing in 'productivity hacks' and hustle culture is optimizing a surface layer; real performance resilience requires developing leaders' emotional regulation, energy management, and self-trust—the internal operating system that determines whether people hold steady or fracture under organizational pressure. Build development programs that address these foundational capacities rather than stacking more tactical efficiency tools.

## Original Story Summary
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I learned to perform under pressure at thirteen, sleeping rough on the street, where reading a room wrong could cost you far more than a meal—sometimes it was the difference between making it through the night and not. So when I tell you that most of what <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/productivity" title="Productivity">productivity</a> culture sells you fails when the moment actually arrives, I&#8217;m not being contrarian for sport. I&#8217;ve watched it fail in stadiums, in boardrooms, and in myself.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">We&#8217;ve built an entire economy around optimizing performance. Morning routines. Focus sprints. Habit stacks. Cold plunges. Breathwork. Most of it is useful, and I use plenty of it. The problem is what the advice quietly promises: that if you install enough of the right habits, you&#8217;ll be ready when it counts.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">You won&#8217;t. And the reason isn&#8217;t that the hacks are too weak. It&#8217;s that they&#8217;re training the wrong layer entirely.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Think of it like a phone. Habits and hacks are apps: useful, satisfying, easy to download. But every app runs on top of an operating system, and no app works when the operating system underneath is weak. Pressure never tests your apps. It tests the operating system. Under pressure, you don&#8217;t rise to your apps. You fall to your code. Every single time.</p>

<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-fighters-and-warriors">Fighters and warriors</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Most high performers are running one of two versions of that code.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The first is the Fighter. Fighters run on urgency, adrenaline, and control. They push through exhaustion and stay on guard, because letting up feels dangerous. Fighters are ferociously effective—right up until they aren&#8217;t.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The second is the Warrior. Warriors prepare for pressure instead of hoping they&#8217;ll rise to it. They shift from calm to intensity and back without losing the thread. They decide which lines they won&#8217;t cross before the moment arrives, so pressure never gets to renegotiate their character in real time.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Fighters react to survive. Warriors prepare to serve. Fighters burn out; warriors endure. A fighter&#8217;s best is a spike—brilliant, unrepeatable, and expensive, paid for in sleep, health, and the people they love. A warrior&#8217;s best is a level they can return to for years, under real weight.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Now, hear me clearly: the fighter is not the enemy. There are moments that demand raw force. The deadline. The crisis. The round you have to win. You want that fire available. But available is not the same as in charge.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Now look honestly at what the optimization industry sells: hustle, grind, attack the day, never let up. That isn&#8217;t a warrior&#8217;s operating system. It&#8217;s fighter technology, packaged and monetized. Productivity culture doesn&#8217;t fail to prepare you for pressure—it makes you a more efficient fighter, then acts surprised when you shatter. An optimized survival mode is still survival mode. You can quote me on that.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I know, because I was a fighter long before I understood the difference. The instincts that kept me alive on the street—braced, scanning, never resting—nearly cost me everything once the street was gone.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I once coached an executive who looked like he was winning: back-to-back meetings, late-night email, travel with no room to recover. He carried it in his jaw and in the way he snapped at people he loved. That&#8217;s what a fighter looks like in a suit. And when he came to me, he wanted a hack. Another app for stress. What he needed was to look underneath.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-the-code-runs-on-three-things">The code runs on three things</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Underneath sits your code, and it runs on energy, emotions, and environment. Not managing them—aligning them.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Energy is whether you can build a charge, hold it, and release it exactly when the moment asks. The fighter runs hot until the tank is empty and calls that commitment. The warrior treats energy as a resource to aim.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Emotions are whether you can read a feeling without letting it write a permanent story. Most people hand the wheel to whatever they&#8217;re feeling. My emotions can ride in the bus with me. I&#8217;m the one driving.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The environment is the rooms you walk into and the one you carry inside: the conversations you allow, the content you consume, the story you tell yourself while everyone is watching. Are you letting the room shape you, or are you shaping it?</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Get those three right, and the warrior has something solid to stand on. Neglect them, and under pressure you default straight back to the fighter—because the fighter is simply what runs when there&#8217;s nothing steadier to hold you.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-what-pressure-actually-reads">What pressure actually reads</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">One thing decides whether that foundation ever gets built: what you do when no one is watching. Every promise you keep to yourself is a deposit of self-trust. Every one you quietly break is a withdrawal. Because in the key moments, that balance sheet shows up. When the stakes spike and some part of you doesn&#8217;t believe your own word, you split. You become two systems fighting each other at the exact moment you need to be one. And what that trust buys you is the thing every founder wants and can&#8217;t purchase: freedom under pressure. Not the absence of nerves—the absence of second-guessing.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Pressure doesn&#8217;t build your character. It reads the file back to you, out loud, in front of everyone.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">So the question productivity culture trains you to ask—what am I doing?—is the wrong one. The better question is: what&#8217;s running underneath what I&#8217;m doing? Where does my energy leak? Am I preparing—or just bracing? What&#8217;s the deeper lie I&#8217;ve been avoiding by staying busy—running app after app so I never have to look?</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In September 2025, Aryna Sabalenka, the world No. 1 I’ve coached for seven years and watched transform from a pure fighter into a true warrior, walked onto Arthur Ashe Stadium against a player who had beaten her at Wimbledon two months earlier. The crowd remembered her old spirals and leaned in, waiting for the repeat. It never came. She didn&#8217;t rush. She didn&#8217;t unravel. She defended her US Open title.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Afterward, reporters asked about my past—the streets, the martial arts, the fighter mentality—and assumed I&#8217;d taught her to fight harder. Her answer stopped me cold. She said I&#8217;d helped her understand what real fighting actually means.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">She didn&#8217;t win by finding another gear of grind. She took the fighter out of the driver&#8217;s seat and put the warrior at the wheel. The fire was still there—fiercer than ever—but it was finally aimed.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">So keep the habits that serve you. Just stop believing they&#8217;re the answer. They&#8217;re apps. Pressure was never going to test your apps. It tests your code.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Build that, and the moment stops owning you.</p>
