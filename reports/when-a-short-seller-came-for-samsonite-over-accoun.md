## Summary
Short seller Blue Orca Capital published a 2018 report accusing luggage maker Samsonite of flawed accounting, hidden related-party conflicts involving the CEO's family, and shaky governance; CEO Ramesh Tainwala resigned after allegations he faked a doctorate, though Samsonite disputed Blue Orca's other claims. The HBS case studies examine the accounting details, the short seller's investigation, and the company's rebuttal.

## Organizational Psychology Angle
The case highlights diffusion of responsibility and authority bias in governance: boards, investors, and auditors often assume 'someone else' (big-name co-investors, Big Four auditors, prestigious boards) has done the due diligence, enabling red flags like credential fraud and related-party transactions to go unchallenged.

## Leadership Lesson
CHROs and COOs should treat credential verification and related-party disclosures as non-negotiable governance baseline checks, not assume reputational signals (big investors, known auditors) substitute for independent diligence, and build board processes that actively interrogate narrative-vs-numbers mismatches rather than accepting a 'good story.'
