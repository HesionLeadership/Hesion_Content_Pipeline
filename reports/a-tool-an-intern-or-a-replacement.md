# A tool, an intern, or a replacement

**Source:** Fast Company  
**Published:** 2026-09-17T12:00:00  
**URL:** [https://www.fastcompany.com/91608419/a-tool-an-intern-or-a-replacement](https://www.fastcompany.com/91608419/a-tool-an-intern-or-a-replacement)

## Summary
AI product builders face a fundamental misalignment: end-users want AI as an assistant to handle tedious tasks, while executives want AI as a headcount replacement to cut costs. Companies like Klarna and Artisan learned the hard way that the 'replacement' narrative sells to investors but fails customers, ultimately reverting to human-AI collaboration models.

## Organizational Psychology Angle
This story illustrates a classic principal-agent misalignment where executive incentives (cost reduction, valuation) diverge from frontline worker needs (task augmentation, job enrichment), creating organizational tension around technology adoption and threatening both employee psychological safety and service quality.

## Leadership Lesson
Before committing to an AI roadmap, CHROs and COOs should explicitly clarify whether the technology is being deployed as a tool, an augmentation, or a replacement—and ensure that framing is transparent and consistent across leadership, frontline teams, and customers. Misalignment between these stakeholders creates costly reversals and erodes trust.

## Original Story Summary
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I was in a pitch meeting with a large food and beverage company a few months ago. Two software audiences in the room—users and managers. Same software product, but with two completely different asks.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The users wanted <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/artificial-intelligence" title="AI">AI</a> to take the parts of their job they hated: manual visual design changes, repetitive reporting, brand validation checks. They wanted an assistant.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Their managers wanted something else. They wanted the tool to free up the team&#8217;s time to reallocate people elsewhere. Either eliminate roles outright, or take a step toward fewer people doing more.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-the-misalignment"><strong>THE MISALIGNMENT</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This is the misalignment. I see it in pitch meetings every day. Users want an AI assistant to make them faster at their own job. Executives want a substitute, something that removes the job entirely, because that&#8217;s the number that moves a valuation. And the startups building AI tools are pricing toward whichever story is more fundable, not whichever one users are asking for.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">You can see this choice: assistant, intern, or employee on pricing pages of various AI startups. Artisan, a sales automation startup, doesn&#8217;t sell seats. It sells &#8220;intern&#8221; and &#8220;employee.&#8221; Two tiers, two job titles, priced to answer the executive&#8217;s question: What is this product going to get me? An assistant or a replacement.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Employees keep describing this gap privately: Teams are told to ship &#8220;AI value&#8221; without a clear sense of who the value is for. IT roadmaps get built around the fundable story, not the useful one. A useful support tool that would make agents faster and customers happier loses the pitch to an &#8220;agent&#8221; that promises to remove humans from the role, even when the services provided by that role are what customers care about. They want to reach a competent human when it matters.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-examples-in-action"><strong>EXAMPLES IN ACTION</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Two companies found that out in public, on opposite ends of the AI market.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Fintech company Klarna announced in <a href="https://www.klarna.com/international/press/klarna-ai-assistant-handles-two-thirds-of-customer-service-chats-in-its-first-month/">2024</a> that its AI assistant was doing the work of 700 agents. Wall Street liked the story. Fourteen months later, the Klarna CEO told <em>Bloomberg</em> the strategy went too far. Cost had been the dominant factor in how the agents were built, and the result was lower quality. Klarna started <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91468582/klarna-tried-to-replace-its-workforce-with-ai">hiring people</a> again, so customers could always reach a human.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In 2024, Artisan began running an in-your-face human replacement story, with billboards across San Francisco and New York reading &#8220;<a href="https://www.artisan.co/blog/stop-hiring-humans">Stop Hiring Humans</a>.&#8221; In August 2026, the company retired the slogan and announced that it was <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/hiring" title="Hiring">hiring</a> its first human business development representative. Artisan’s founder wrote that the billboard and the new hire were both the right call, and that “the future is humans and AI, not humans or AI.”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Neither company changed its mind about AI. Both changed their mind about who the product was for. The substitute story sold well to investors and the press. The sidekick story is what customers actually wanted, and both companies came back to it once the gap became too expensive to ignore.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">As an executive building or buying AI right now, that&#8217;s the question worth asking before the roadmap hardens around whichever story gets funded. What did we actually build or buy here: a tool, an intern, or a replacement? Is it priced that way? And does the person using it every day know it too?</p>
