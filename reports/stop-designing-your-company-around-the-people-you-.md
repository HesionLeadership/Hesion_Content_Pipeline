# Stop designing your company around the people you have

**Source:** Fast Company  
**Published:** 2026-07-27T09:50:00  
**URL:** [https://www.fastcompany.com/91570773/stop-designing-your-company-around-the-people-you-have](https://www.fastcompany.com/91570773/stop-designing-your-company-around-the-people-you-have)

## Summary
The article argues that founder-led companies often design their organizational structure around existing people rather than around the mission, which limits scalability. It advocates for designing the future-state organization first, then mapping people to roles, identifying gaps, and building governance structures.

## Organizational Psychology Angle
This directly addresses the tension between person-centric and structure-centric organizational design, reflecting research on how loyalty biases and role ambiguity in founder-led firms create structural debt that inhibits scaling. The concept of 'designing around people' mirrors person-organization fit literature but inverts it—showing what happens when the organization conforms to individuals rather than vice versa.

## Leadership Lesson
CHROs and COOs should periodically conduct a 'nameless org chart' exercise—designing the ideal organizational structure based solely on strategic objectives before mapping current talent to roles. This reveals structural gaps, over-concentrated responsibilities, and misalignments that are invisible when planning starts with incumbent personnel. Pair this with clear governance rhythms (meeting cadences, KPIs, reporting structures) to shift from founder-dependent to scalable operations.

## Original Story Summary
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">One of the most common challenges I see in founder-led companies appears right after success.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The business is growing, revenue is strong, customers are happy, and the team is lean and hardworking. In many cases, the founder has been able to build a real business with far fewer people than a larger company would ever consider possible.&nbsp;<br />&nbsp;<br />Then comes the next ambition: they want to double the business, enter new markets, create more predictability, or simply spend less time working in the business and more time working on it. The founder may also want something very human: more private time or family time and more breathing room.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Recently, I worked with the founder of a highly profitable company that had been operating successfully for nearly two decades. Like many entrepreneurs, he had built an extraordinary business with a remarkably lean team. The company was generating strong margins, growth opportunities were everywhere, and yet the conversation wasn&#8217;t about revenue. It was about time.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;How do I get out of the weeds?&#8221; he asked. &#8220;How do I spend more time on strategy and less time managing everything myself?&#8221;&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">It&#8217;s a great question and one I hear often. The answer, however, is rarely “better <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/productivity" title="Productivity">time management</a>”. More often than not, it&#8217;s an organizational design issue.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-start-with-the-mission-not-the-org-chart-nbsp-nbsp">Start With The Mission, Not The Org Chart&nbsp;<strong> &nbsp;</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Most founders begin these conversations by walking me through their people.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">They tell me who runs operations, who handles finance, who has been with them since the beginning, and who wears three different hats because that&#8217;s what was required when the company was smaller.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I understand why that happens. These are the people who helped build the company. They are known, trusted, and often deeply committed. But before making recommendations, I often see the same pattern: the organization has been designed around the people currently in the business instead of around the structure required to achieve the mission.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That works for a while. It may even be necessary early on. But eventually, it becomes limiting.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Before talking about people, we need to talk about the mission.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">What is the company trying to achieve over the next 18 to 36 months? What growth is expected? What strategic priorities matter most? What capabilities will be required to get there?&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Once those questions are answered, the major functions of the business begin to reveal themselves. Depending on the company, those might include finance, customer experience, technology, strategic partnerships, operations, <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/branding" title="Branding">branding</a>, international expansion, or product development. Every organization is different, but every organization has a handful of critical functions that will determine whether it succeeds or falls short of its goals.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Only after those functions are clear should we start discussing people.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-build-the-future-organization-before-assigning-names-nbsp-nbsp-nbsp">Build The Future Organization Before Assigning Names&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">One of the most effective exercises I use is surprisingly simple. Take a whiteboard, digital or physical, and design the future-state organization without any names attached to it.&nbsp;&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Do this without politics or loyalty considerations. No “but she has been with us forever,” or “he is the only one who understands that customer.”&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">You simply ask: If we were building this company today to accomplish our mission over the next three years, what would the organization need to look like?&nbsp;<br />&nbsp;<br />Start at the top. What are the major functions that require senior ownership? What departments need leadership? What capabilities must exist if the company is going to grow the way the founder says it must grow?&nbsp;&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Then move down into the middle and lower layers of the organization. What support is needed? What roles need to exist? What responsibilities need to be separated, combined, or elevated?&nbsp;&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">As the structure takes shape, the conversation becomes much more objective. You stop talking about personalities and start talking about responsibilities. Instead of debating individuals, you start focusing on what the business actually requires.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Only after the future organization is designed do you begin bringing the names and people back into the conversation.&nbsp;<br />&nbsp;<br />I like to use sticky notes, with each note having one person’s name on it. Once the ideal future-state structure is on the board, you start placing people where they truly fit.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Sarah fits here and James fits there. This person may be able to grow into that role, that person is currently carrying two responsibilities that probably need to be separated.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">And then, inevitably, you will find two things: gaps and people who do not clearly fit. You will see that functions that are critical to future growth may not have leadership at all. You will also see people that may be successful today but not ideally positioned for where the company is headed.&nbsp;&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That&#8217;s where the real work begins.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-the-empty-boxes-tell-the-real-story-nbsp-nbsp">The Empty Boxes Tell The Real Story&nbsp;&nbsp;</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">One of the most revealing moments in this exercise is identifying the positions that don’t exist.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Founders are often surprised to discover that every empty box on an organization chart already has an owner, whether they intended it or not. If there is no head of technology, someone is making technology decisions. If there is no finance leader, someone is carrying responsibility for financial planning. If there is no person leading strategic partnerships, customer experience, or operations, those responsibilities still exist.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In founder-led businesses, these functions almost always flow back to the founder.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This is why so many entrepreneurs feel trapped in the day-to-day. They aren&#8217;t just running a company but are often acting as the unofficial leader of multiple departments at the same time.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Once those gaps become visible, priorities become clearer. Which positions need to be filled now? Which can be developed internally? Which can wait? The conversation shifts from feeling overwhelmed to creating a roadmap.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That prioritization matters. You do not need to build a big corporate structure overnight. In fact, most entrepreneurial companies should not. But you do need to understand which gaps are slowing growth, creating risk, and keeping the founder trapped in the day-to-day.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-don-t-design-around-your-best-people-nbsp-nbsp">Don&#8217;t Design Around Your Best People&nbsp;&nbsp;</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I had a conversation recently with a senior executive at a large company about a leader within his technology organization. In one business unit, the role was responsible for a single function. In another, someone with similar skills was overseeing multiple areas.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">His argument was: &#8220;That person can handle both.&#8221;&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Maybe they can. But that&#8217;s not the right question.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The question isn&#8217;t whether someone is capable of carrying two responsibilities but whether that&#8217;s the best structure for the business. Too often, organizations are designed around the strengths of the people who happen to be there rather than around what the company actually needs.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I&#8217;ve seen this happen in organizations of every size. A founder keeps someone in a role because they&#8217;ve been loyal. A leader expands a position because a particular employee is exceptionally capable. An executive avoids <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/hiring" title="Hiring">hiring</a> because one person is willing to absorb more work.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Sometimes those decisions are reasonable, even necessary. But over time, they can create organizations that reflect the people instead of the mission.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Do not design the organization around individual capability alone. Design it around what the business requires and then decide whether your current people can be developed, repositioned, or replaced to meet that requirement.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-structure-creates-scale-nbsp">Structure Creates Scale&nbsp;</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Once the right structure is in place, the next step is governance.&nbsp;&nbsp;<br />&nbsp;<br />This is where I often see entrepreneurial companies struggle. They may have strong culture, relationships, and hustle, but not enough operating rhythm.&nbsp;&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Each department or function needs a clear mission. Each should have measurable goals and KPIs, know what success looks like over a defined period of time. The goals should be specific, measurable, achievable, realistic, and time-based. <br /> <br />But goals alone are not enough. The CEO also needs a governance model.  </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">What gets reported weekly by email? How often do one-on-ones happen? When does the leadership team meet? When does each functional team meet? When does the entire company come together? Is there a monthly or quarterly business review? A semiannual planning session? An annual strategic off-site?&nbsp;&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Many founders worry that these rhythms sound too corporate, but they are not bureaucracy when done well. They are the operating system that allows the founder to stop carrying the entire business in their head. Without structure, every decision flows back to the founder. With structure, leaders throughout the organization can make decisions, solve problems, and drive results without constant intervention.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That&#8217;s the difference between a company that is founder-dependent and a company that is scalable.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-build-for-where-you-re-going-nbsp">Build For Where You&#8217;re Going&nbsp;</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The most successful founders I work with eventually make the same shift. They stop asking where their current people fit and start asking what organization their mission requires.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That doesn&#8217;t mean ignoring the people who helped build the business. It simply means recognizing that growth often requires different structures, capabilities, and sometimes difficult decisions.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A company cannot double in size if every important decision, customer issue, hiring choice, and strategic initiative still runs through one person. At some point, the organization has to be designed for where it&#8217;s going, not where it&#8217;s been.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That is the real shift. Stop asking, “Where do my current people fit?” and start asking “What organization does our mission require?”&nbsp;&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Then build toward that with honesty, discipline, and courage.</p>
