# Like working from home? In Australia, it could become the law

**Source:** Fast Company  
**Published:** 2026-09-08T20:00:00  
**URL:** [https://www.fastcompany.com/91603687/like-working-from-home-in-australia-it-could-become-the-law](https://www.fastcompany.com/91603687/like-working-from-home-in-australia-it-could-become-the-law)

## Summary
Australia's Victoria state is considering legislation that would grant eligible employees a blanket right to work from home two days per week, shifting the burden onto employers to justify denying remote work. This represents an escalation beyond 'right to request' laws in the UK, Ireland, and New Zealand, and stands in tension with the global corporate trend of tightening return-to-office mandates.

## Organizational Psychology Angle
This story highlights the fundamental tension between organizational autonomy in workplace design and externally imposed flexibility mandates, raising questions about whether legislated remote work rights enhance employee well-being and retention or constrain managerial discretion in ways that undermine organizational effectiveness.

## Leadership Lesson
CHROs and COOs should proactively design flexible work policies that balance employee autonomy with business needs, because if organizations fail to offer genuine flexibility voluntarily, governments may impose rigid mandates that remove managerial discretion entirely. Building a culture of trust and transparent decision-making around work location can preempt regulatory intervention.

## Original Story Summary
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In some places, the next battle in the war over remote work may be fought in legislatures, not HR departments.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The United Kingdom, Ireland, and New Zealand have already passed laws giving workers the right to request remote work and to have the request considered fairly by their employers. Canada extends similar rights to federal employees. The European Union does so for parents or caregivers of young children. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Now, the Australian state of Victoria is <a href="https://www.vgso.vic.gov.au/proposed-new-victorian-work-home-wfh-laws" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">considering</a> taking things a step further. There, lawmakers are weighing a proposal to establish a blanket right for eligible employees to work from home two days a week. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">If enacted, the measure would represent a noticeable escalation in the push to enshrine workplace flexibility in law: Instead of merely giving employees the right to ask for remote work, Victoria would effectively shift the burden onto employers to justify why an eligible worker <em>cannot</em> work remotely.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That distinction is important. Most remote work laws adopted elsewhere stop well short of guaranteeing that employees can work from their home offices or kitchen tables. Instead, they establish a process: Workers can ask for a flexible arrangement without being dismissed out of hand, and employers generally must consider the request and provide an explanation if they reject it.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In the U.K., employees have had a legal right to request flexible working for years, and <a href="https://worknest.com/us/blog/the-future-of-flexible-working-recent-changes-and-whats-coming-next" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">reforms that took effect in 2024</a> made that right available from an employee’s first day on the job. <a href="https://www.citizensinformation.ie/en/employment/employment-rights-and-conditions/hours-of-work/right-to-request-flexible-working/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Ireland’s 2024 rules</a> similarly created a formal right to request remote work, although employers can still turn down requests after considering factors such as the nature of the job and the potential impact on the business.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Victoria’s proposal would move closer to treating remote work as a right, as opposed to a benefit granted at an employer’s discretion. That makes the debate there particularly relevant at a moment when many large employers are moving in the opposite direction.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Across the corporate world, companies that embraced remote work during the pandemic have <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91392977/is-the-return-to-office-push-backfiring-on-employers-retrun-to-work-office-policy-debate" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">tightened attendance requirements</a>, increased the number of mandatory office days, and in some cases <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91229797/what-companies-are-getting-wrong-about-return-to-office" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">ordered employees back five days a week</a>.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The result is an emerging divide over who ultimately gets to decide where work happens: individual companies or governments. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The proposal in Victoria has already sparked a <a href="https://www.news.com.au/finance/work/battle-erupts-over-victorias-work-from-home-right-as-lawyers-find-proposed-laws-are-unconstitutional/news-story/d0316bac23a34010bb571eeb6c7f31f8?utm_source=chatgpt.com" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">fierce political and legal fight</a>, with <a href="https://www.smartcompany.com.au/industrial-relations/businesses-call-to-scrap-victorias-wfh-plan-after-consultation-stalls/?utm_source=chatgpt.com" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">business groups</a> warning that it would intrude on employers’ ability to manage their workplaces and <a href="https://www.heraldsun.com.au/news/victoria/victorias-new-work-from-home-laws-doomed-in-high-court-lawyers-warn/news-story/a29ae7b6246ac419073aaddb058a5991?utm_source=chatgpt.com" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">critics questioning</a> whether the state has the constitutional authority to impose such a mandate. How that fight plays out could help determine whether other governments see remote work as a workplace perk or a right worth protecting.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Could the trend toward enshrining remote work in law someday extend to the United States?&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Don’t hold your breath, Rachel Arnow-Richman, a professor at the University of Florida&#8217;s Levin College of Law, told <em>Fast Company</em>. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Aside from the most progressive states, like Vermont—which has had a law in place <a href="https://www.disabilityleavelaw.com/2013/06/articles/leave-policies/vermont-employees-may-request-flexible-work-arrangement/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">since 2013</a> that gives workers the right to request flexible work arrangements, including working from home—it is unlikely that legislators will be able to pass remote work laws any time soon. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">“Broadly speaking, [remote work] is a business issue, not a legal issue” in the United States, Arnow-Richman said, noting that the U.S. is largely unique among Western, industrialized countries in having “at-will” employment—i.e., allowing employers to fire workers without cause. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The U.S. is also far behind most Western, industrialized countries in adopting labor protections like paid sick leave and paid family leave, she added. This means pro-labor legislators have more important issues to address before they can get to remote work.</p>
