# 5 tips for staying calm when you’re stressed

**Source:** Fast Company  
**Published:** 2026-08-01T05:00:00  
**URL:** [https://www.fastcompany.com/91581831/5-tips-for-staying-calm-when-youre-stressed](https://www.fastcompany.com/91581831/5-tips-for-staying-calm-when-youre-stressed)

## Summary
The article presents 5 practices for staying calm under stress: (1) Name feelings objectively without drama, (2) Practice regulating your nervous system, (3) Focus on your sphere of control, (4) Reconnect to something greater than yourself, and (5) Stay connected with people who help you do the first four things. It emphasizes that stress causes people to revert to old behavioral patterns, which can undermine organizational culture-building efforts.

## Organizational Psychology Angle
Stress-induced behavioral regression threatens organizational culture investments by causing leaders and team members to abandon newly developed norms and revert to protective, counterproductive patterns. Self-regulation and social support systems are critical infrastructure for sustaining culture change through periods of organizational pressure.

## Leadership Lesson
CHROs and COOs should recognize that culture transformation is most vulnerable during high-stress periods—build organizational systems that normalize self-regulation practices, maintain accountability relationships, and help leaders notice and recover from stress-driven behavioral regression before it cascades through teams.

## Original Story Summary
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">When stress rises, most people do not suddenly become someone different. They often become more intensely who they are or were rather than who they are working on becoming. Old habits, protective behaviors, and familiar patterns tend to reappear, sometimes before we even realize it is happening. A leader who normally listens well may become controlling. A collaborative team member may withdraw. A person who values calm communication may become reactive or defensive.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In moments of uncertainty, pressure, or exhaustion, people often revert to what feels familiar and protective, even when those behaviors no longer serve them or the people around them.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This is one reason culture work can feel fragile during stressful seasons. Organizations may spend months building trust, improving communication, and creating healthier norms, only to watch old dynamics quickly reemerge under pressure. But stress does not erase growth. More often, it reveals where deeper practice and support are still needed.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Here are five practices that can help people and teams avoid reverting to old behaviors during difficult moments.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-1-name-how-you-are-feeling-in-objective-terms-without-drama">1. Name how you are feeling in objective terms without drama</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">One of the fastest ways to lose self-awareness under stress is to become fused with emotion instead of observing it.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Instead of saying, “Everything is falling apart,” a more objective observation might sound like:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>“I notice I am becoming defensive.”</li>



<li>“I feel overwhelmed and I am losing curiosity.”</li>



<li>“I can feel myself trying to control outcomes.”</li>



<li>“I am shutting down instead of engaging.”</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Objectively naming our internal state creates just enough space between stimulus and reaction for better choices to become possible—and it helps others understand what is going on for us so they can be helpful.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-2-practice-regulating-your-nervous-system">2. Practice regulating your nervous system</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">When people are stressed, the nervous system often interprets situations as threats even when no physical danger exists. Once this happens, the body can shift into fight, flight, freeze, or fawn responses that significantly affect behavior, communication, and decision-making.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Many of the behaviors people regret later are not simply mindset problems and they are rarely “character flaws.” More often, they are simply nervous system responses.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This is why regulation matters so much. The ability to calm and stabilize ourselves is not separate from leadership or culture work. It is central to it.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Regulation practices do not need to be complicated. Sometimes the most effective approaches are surprisingly simple:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>Take slow, intentional breaths</li>



<li>Go for a walk before responding to an email</li>



<li>Pause before speaking in a difficult meeting</li>



<li>Sleep adequately</li>



<li>Reduce overstimulation and distraction in your environment</li>



<li>Practice mindfulness or prayer</li>



<li>Move your body regularly</li>



<li>Spend time outside</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">When individuals learn how to regulate themselves more consistently, they are less likely to spread stress throughout a team or organization. Organizations that normalize recovery, reflection, and healthy pacing create conditions where people are capable of responding thoughtfully rather than reactively.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-3-focus-on-your-sphere-of-control">3. Focus on your sphere of control</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Stress often intensifies when people become consumed by things they cannot control. At difficult times, it is easy to obsess over other people’s behavior, uncertain outcomes, organizational decisions, or broader circumstances. While some awareness of these realities is important, excessive focus on them can create helplessness and emotional exhaustion.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A healthier practice is to repeatedly return attention to one question: “What is actually mine to do right now?” Sometimes the answer is small:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>Clarify expectations</li>



<li>Have one honest conversation</li>



<li>Follow through on a commitment</li>



<li>Ask a thoughtful question</li>



<li>Admit a mistake</li>



<li>Take the next right step</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This practice grounds people in agency instead of anxiety. Healthy cultures are built through consistent everyday behaviors, not through waiting for perfect circumstances or other people to change. People regain stability when they focus on the actions, choices, and responses that remain within their influence.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-4-reconnect-often-to-something-greater-than-yourself">4. Reconnect often to something greater than yourself</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Stress narrows perspective. People can become consumed by immediate problems, personal frustrations, or fear-based thinking. In these moments, reconnecting to something larger can help restore clarity and resilience.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For some people, this may involve faith, spirituality, or prayer. For others, it may involve purpose, service, family, community, or a commitment to meaningful work.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The important question is: “What helps you remember why this matters?”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Purpose acts as an anchor during difficult seasons. It reminds people that discomfort, uncertainty, and challenge are often part of growth, not evidence that growth is failing. This is especially important for leaders. Teams are deeply influenced by whether leaders remain connected to a larger purpose during stressful moments or become consumed by fear, ego, or self-protection.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Healthy culture requires people who can continually reconnect to values and purpose even when pressure rises.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-5-stay-connected-with-the-people-who-help-you-do-the-first-four-things">5. Stay connected with the people who help you do the first four things </h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Stress often drives people toward isolation. Ironically, isolation is usually the very thing that makes old behaviors more likely to return. Most people need others who can help them stay grounded, honest, and self-aware. These are the people who:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>Help you notice when you are below the line</li>



<li>Encourage healthier perspectives</li>



<li>Remind you of your values</li>



<li>Support nervous system regulation</li>



<li>Challenge you compassionately</li>



<li>Help you reconnect to purpose</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Stress often amplifies existing patterns and exposes where deeper awareness, support, and practice are still needed. The goal is not to navigate pressure perfectly or never feel dysregulated. The goal is to notice sooner, recover more quickly, and stay connected to the people, practices, and purposes that help us respond intentionally instead of reactively. This is the ongoing work of healthy culture: not eliminating stress, but learning how to move through it without abandoning who we are trying to become.</p>
