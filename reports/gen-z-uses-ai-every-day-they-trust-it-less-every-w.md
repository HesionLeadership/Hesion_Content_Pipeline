# Gen Z uses AI every day. They trust it less every week

**Source:** Fast Company  
**Published:** 2026-07-28T08:30:00  
**URL:** [https://www.fastcompany.com/91577499/gen-z-ai-every-day-less-trust](https://www.fastcompany.com/91577499/gen-z-ai-every-day-less-trust)

## Summary
Gen Z leads AI adoption in the workplace but simultaneously distrusts it more each week, creating a dangerous gap between usage metrics and genuine buy-in that organizations are failing to detect.

## Organizational Psychology Angle
The story reveals a critical disconnect between behavioral compliance (tool usage) and attitudinal commitment (trust/belief), a well-documented phenomenon in organizational psychology where mandated adoption without psychological buy-in leads to resistance, workarounds, and even sabotage. The 'usage without trust' pattern mirrors research on forced compliance and organizational cynicism.

## Leadership Lesson
CHROs and COOs should stop equating tool adoption metrics with successful change management—track trust and sentiment alongside usage data, treat growing gaps as early-warning signals, and build structured feedback channels with younger employees who may be complying behaviorally while harboring deep skepticism that will eventually surface as turnover, resistance, or sabotage.

## Strength Score
**8/10** — This story directly addresses technology adoption and change management within organizations, touching on trust, employee resistance, the gap between behavioral compliance and attitudinal acceptance, and how leaders can misread adoption dashboards. The practical recommendations (trust metrics, structured dissent channels, treating young hires as early-warning systems) are grounded in legitimate organizational psychology concerns. The one limitation is that it's partly a consumer/product story, but the organizational implications are substantial and well-articulated.

## Original Story Summary
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Earlier this year, we showed two of our interns, both bright young people in their twenties, an early build of an <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/artificial-intelligence" title="AI">AI</a>-generated presenter we were developing for training content. I expected them to take to her instantly. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">These are young people who grew up on social media and inside video games, people who had never known a world without smartphones and YouTube. They were the archetypal digital natives. If anyone would be comfortable with an AI avatar, surely it was them.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I was wrong. To put it bluntly, they hated the build, and they hated it <em>because</em> it was AI.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Our interns are not outliers. They are symptoms of a wider trend across Gen Z. Just this spring, former Google CEO <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/eric-schmidt">Eric Schmidt</a> was <a href="https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/former-google-ceo-booed-graduation-speech-ai-rcna345585">booed repeatedly</a> while delivering a commencement address about AI at the University of Arizona, pausing mid-speech to acknowledge the crowd: “I know what many of you are feeling about that. I can hear you.” </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This was no freak occurrence—Schmidt was the third commencement speaker booed just that month for praising AI, after speakers at the <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/05/14/style/ucf-commencement-ai-booed-gloria-caulfield.html">University of Central Florida</a> and <a href="https://www.npr.org/2026/05/20/nx-s1-5822419/ai-colleges-commencement-booing">Middle Tennessee State</a> met the same reception.</p>




<p></p>


<p class="wp-block-paragraph">According to the latest <a href="https://news.gallup.com/poll/708224/gen-adoption-steady-skepticism-climbs.aspx">Voices of Gen Z survey</a>, young Americans’ feelings about AI are souring fast. The share of 14- to 29-year-olds who feel hopeful about the technology has slumped to 18% in 2026 from 27% a year earlier. And these are the employees your AI rollout assumes will lead adoption, the customers your product roadmap is counting on, and, within a decade, the buyers who will decide which vendors to trust.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">When the demographic at the center of every AI adoption model turns openly hostile to the pitch, that is a strategy problem that business leaders urgently need to address.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-gen-z-and-ai">Gen Z and AI</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Gen Z workers, we’ve been told, <a href="https://peoplemanagingpeople.com/learning-development/how-gen-z-will-drag-companies-into-the-ai-age/">trust AI more than their own managers</a> for career advice. They aren’t just embracing the technology, <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2025/09/12/iwg-report-gen-z-employees-are-coaching-older-colleagues-to-use-ai.html">a widely covered industry survey announced</a>; they’re driving its adoption—coaching their older colleagues along. And organizations have planned accordingly: rollouts aimed at the young, training budgets that presume fluency, product launches that treat under-30 adoption as the given from which everything else follows. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Call it the digital-native assumption: Put technology in front of someone who grew up on screens, and adoption takes care of itself.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This adoption story is half-right, which is precisely its danger. Because the fundamental problem here is not rejection. Rather, it is something much more slippery: usage without trust. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A <a href="https://hbr.org/2026/01/how-gen-z-uses-gen-ai-and-why-it-worries-them">recent survey</a> of nearly 2,500 young adults, led by researchers at the University of Pennsylvania, found that Gen Z leads the workplace in AI adoption while also believing the technology is making their colleagues lazier and less capable. A 2026 survey of more than 27,000 workers by the recruitment giant Randstad reports that “Gen Z is the most concerned generation” when it comes to the impact of AI on their jobs.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Navigating this new reality means understanding three things: why this generation’s distrust is informed rather than reflexive, why standard metrics fail to detect it, and how it is redefining what counts as a viable product.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-proximity-breeds-scrutiny">Proximity breeds scrutiny</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The generation that grew up inside the technology isn’t the most devoted to it—it’s the most discriminating about it. And the reason lies in that generation’s vantage point. They’ve watched classmates outsource essays and lose the ability to write them, seen synthetic content flood the feeds they live in, and entered a job market where the entry-level rungs they were promised are the first being automated away. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Their distrust isn’t rooted in reflexive anti-tech anxiety. It is grounded in the lessons their real-world experiences have taught them.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For organizational leaders, the takeaway is simple: Whether you’re a school integrating AI into classrooms, a manager pushing tools on your team, or a founder pitching an AI product, you can no longer assume the digitally native generation will gravitate toward it. Gen Z acceptance is a hypothesis to be tested rather than a fact to be relied upon. The digital native, as the roadmaps tend to imagine them, no longer exists.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-usage-is-an-incomplete-metric">Usage is an incomplete metric</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Roughly <a href="https://news.gallup.com/poll/708224/gen-adoption-steady-skepticism-climbs.aspx">51% of Gen Z uses generative AI</a> at least weekly in the U.S., and <a href="https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/products-eurostat-news/w/edn-20260210-1">64% of 16- to 24-year-olds used AI in 2025 in the EU</a>. If you simply looked at adoption stats, you’d think that the tale of Gen Z and AI was a love story that was steadily deepening.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">But love stories can go rotten from the inside, and that’s what is happening here. Even daily users are markedly less excited and less hopeful than they were a year ago. And the gap between use and belief shows up in behavior, not just sentiment. This is how organizations get adoption without buy-in, opportunistic use without conviction, work-arounds that bypass official systems, and, sometimes, open resistance.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For executives, the workplace translation is blunt. The employee who dutifully uses your mandated AI tool every day and trusts it a little less every week is not an adoption win. They’re deferred churn, at best. At worst, they may be part of the <a href="https://go.writer.com/hubfs/pdfs/ai-adoption-survey-2026-wpi.pdf?hsLang=en">nearly one-third of workers who already admit</a> to quietly attempting to sabotage the AI transformation itself. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The answer to this challenge is to track trust, not just usage, by adding sentiment tracking to every AI rollout dashboard.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-privacy-is-a-product-feature">Privacy is a product feature</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Nowhere does the collision between the digital-native assumption and digital-native reality play out more visibly than with <a href="https://tech.yahoo.com/wearables/article/why-is-everyone-freaking-out-about-smart-glasses-150000123.html">Meta’s AI-powered smart glasses</a>.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">On paper, camera-equipped, always-listening eyewear should be a Gen Z product: wearable, social, ambient, and gamified. In practice, the core objection—<a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2025/08/27/gen-z-smart-glasses-digital-privacy-consent/">raised most sharply by young consumers</a>—is that the glasses barge into personal privacy, a worry only sharpened by <a href="https://fortune.com/2026/03/27/meta-smart-glasses-filming-watching-workers-lawsuit-privacy/">reports of how footage from the glasses gets reviewed and used</a>. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">This generation has spent its whole life learning that privacy is the cost of technology, and so Gen Z sees a threat where Meta may have hoped it saw a cool feature.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For business leaders, that means privacy should move from being a compliance issue to becoming a market question. This means making the ethics review a commercial gate rather than a legal one —a genuine go/no-go decision, with young consumers and employees in the room.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-four-moves-to-make-this-quarter">Four moves to make this quarter</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Here are four moves that will help you make your business Gen Z-ready:</p>



<ol class="wp-block-list">
<li><strong>Audit your AI roadmap for digital-native assumptions.</strong> Flag every line where adoption is projected from age rather than evidenced by testing.</li>



<li><strong>Add trust metrics to every adoption dashboard.</strong> Track sentiment alongside usage, and treat a widening gap between the two as an early-warning signal, not a footnote.</li>



<li><strong>Run the “Should we, really?” gate.</strong> Before any AI feature ships, convene a review that treats privacy and surveillance concerns as market risks with veto power.</li>



<li><strong>Rewire your youngest hires as an early-warning system.</strong> Build structured channels to surface the skepticism of your youngest hires—it’s the closest thing you have to a preview of your future market.</li>
</ol>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-the-first-generation-to-see-clearly">The first generation to see clearly</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The temptation is to read Gen Z’s disillusionment as a generational quirk—kids being contrarian about the tools they’ll inevitably embrace. The evidence points the other way. Today’s twentysomethings are not simply younger versions of the thirtysomethings ahead of them; their political instincts, their values, and their relationship with technology resonate on a genuinely different frequency. So profound is this shift that some observers detect the early stirrings of the kind of countercultural shift not seen since the 1960s.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">And as go the young, so goes society: The ambivalence of Gen Z isn’t a lag in the adoption curve. It is a preview of where your entire market is heading. Companies that keep designing for the mythical digital native will keep shipping products the real one resents.</p>
