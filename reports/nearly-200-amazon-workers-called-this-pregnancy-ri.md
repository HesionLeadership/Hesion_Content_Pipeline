# Nearly 200 Amazon workers called this pregnancy rights hotline in 2026. Now there’s a lawsuit

**Source:** Fast Company  
**Published:** 2026-09-08T20:00:00  
**URL:** [https://www.fastcompany.com/91603561/nearly-200-amazon-workers-called-this-pregnancy-rights-hotline-in-2026-now-theres-a-lawsuit](https://www.fastcompany.com/91603561/nearly-200-amazon-workers-called-this-pregnancy-rights-hotline-in-2026-now-theres-a-lawsuit)

## Summary
A class action lawsuit alleges Amazon systematically failed to accommodate pregnant warehouse workers, requiring doctor's notes for basic accommodations and retaliating against those who exercised their rights under the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act. Nearly 200 Amazon workers called a pregnancy rights helpline in 2026, and the EEOC has found reasonable cause to believe Amazon discriminated against a nationwide class of female warehouse associates.

## Organizational Psychology Angle
This case illustrates a systemic gap between espoused organizational values ('best employer, safest place to work') and enacted policies, reflecting a compliance-oriented rather than care-oriented approach to worker accommodations. The pattern suggests institutional rigidity in large-scale operations where productivity metrics may override employee well-being protocols, creating a culture where frontline managers default to punitive rather than supportive responses.

## Leadership Lesson
CHROs and COOs must ensure that accommodation policies are designed for ease of access rather than bureaucratic gatekeeping—requiring medical documentation for basic, well-established pregnancy accommodations creates unnecessary barriers that expose the organization to legal liability and erode trust. Leaders should audit the gap between accommodation approval rates (Amazon claims 99.9%) and actual frontline worker experiences, as aggregate metrics can mask systemic failures in policy implementation.

## Original Story Summary
<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><a href="http://www.fastcompany.com/section/amazon" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Amazon</a> is once again under fire for its work policies, this time with a new class action lawsuit filed against the retail giant on Tuesday that alleges the company has failed to make basic workplace accommodations for its <a href="http://www.fastcompany.com/section/pregnancy" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">pregnant</a> employees.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The <a href="https://www.pacermonitor.com/public/filings/DKB3LWGY/Barclay_et_al_v_Amazoncom_Services_LLC__nyedce-26-05531__0001.0.pdf" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">lawsuit</a>, filed in a Brooklyn federal court, alleges that the Seattle-based company has engaged in a systematic and company-wide failure to accommodate pregnant workers and deliberately retaliated against them. The four named plaintiffs, all of whom were formerly employed at Amazon warehouses in New York, detail instances in which they claim they were denied work accommodations and subsequently disciplined or terminated, or faced retaliation during their pregnancies. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The Pregnant Workers Fairness Act, which was signed into law by President <a href="http://www.fastcompany.com/section/joe-biden" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Joe Biden</a> in 2022 and went into effect in 2023, is key to this proposed nationwide <a href="http://www.fastcompany.com/section/class-action-lawsuit" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">class action lawsuit</a>. That <a href="https://www.eeoc.gov/wysk/what-you-should-know-about-pregnant-workers-fairness-act" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">legislation</a> requires employers to provide “reasonable accommodations” for qualified employees affected by pregnancy, childbirth, or related medical conditions.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The new lawsuit alleges that Amazon’s policies violated the law in two ways—by requiring a pregnant worker to provide a doctor’s note before the company will provide even basic accommodations and by failing to account for legally protected pregnancy-related breaks. The plaintiffs are represented by Emery Celli Brinckerhoff Abady Ward &amp; Maazel LLP, a civil rights law firm, and A Better Balance, a nonprofit advocacy organization, both of which have previously been involved in legislation against the retail giant.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">“As our lawsuit details, Amazon regularly violates the law. Under its policies, pregnant workers are not protected, and they face retaliation for exercising their rights,” Debbie Greenberger, a partner at Emery Celli Brinckerhoff Abady Ward &amp; Maazel LLP, said in a <a href="https://www.abetterbalance.org/nationwide-class-action-against-amazon-claims-abusive-treatment-of-pregnant-warehouse-workers/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">statement</a>. “Too often, pregnant women working at Amazon warehouses have to risk their health or their pregnancy just to do their jobs.”&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">An Amazon spokesperson told <em>Fast Company</em> the company conducted “thorough” internal reviews of each of the cases referenced in the <a href="http://www.fastcompany.com/section/lawsuits" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">lawsuit</a> and noted that some of the details laid out in the suit contain inaccuracies and omit important details, but didn&#8217;t provide specifics. The company also noted that in the past year, 99.9% of pregnancy accommodation requests have been approved.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;Ensuring the health and well-being of our employees is one of our greatest responsibilities, and we strive to provide a safe and supportive environment for everyone, which includes supporting tens of thousands of employees with pregnancy accommodations each year,” Kelly Nantel, an Amazon spokesperson, said in a statement to <em>Fast Company</em>.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-amazon-s-policies-under-scrutiny">AMAZON’S POLICIES UNDER SCRUTINY</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Amazon states on its <a href="https://www.aboutamazon.com/what-we-do" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">website</a> that it has a mission to be “Earth’s most customer-centric company, best employer, and safest place to work.” But a long list of lawsuits and other complaints over the years have contested the latter two points, in particular.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">There are currently dozens of open cases against the company with the <a href="http://www.fastcompany.com/section/nlrb" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">National Labor Relations Board</a> and the <a href="http://www.fastcompany.com/section/eeoc" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC)</a> for a broad range of alleged violations against workers. And the new lawsuit is hardly the first time that its treatment of pregnant workers has come under scrutiny.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In October 2025, New Jersey filed a <a href="http://www.fastcompany.com/section/civil-rights" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">civil rights</a> lawsuit against Amazon, alleging it systematically violated state civil rights and pregnancy protection laws across its fulfillment centers, while New York made a similar <a href="https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-announces-complaint-filed-against-amazon#:~:text=Governor%20Kathy%20Hochul%20today%20announced%20the%20New,them%20to%20work%20with%20a%20reasonable%20accommodation." rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">complaint</a> in 2022 that the company was engaging in discrimination against pregnant workers by denying them reasonable accommodations.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Earlier this year, the EEOC determined that Amazon had violated both the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act and the Americans With Disabilities Act. In February, the commission determined that the company had discriminated against Jennifer Hatch, one of the plaintiffs in the class action lawsuit, by failing to provide her with pregnancy-related accommodations.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That same determination noted there was “reasonable cause” to believe the company had likewise discriminated against “a nationwide class of female warehouse associates” since 2023. The three other plaintiffs in the lawsuit similarly filed complaints against Amazon with the EEOC.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-a-bigger-threat">A BIGGER THREAT</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph" id="h-as-the-new-lawsuit-notes-the-four-plaintiffs-are-likely-indicative-of-a-bigger-problem-with-how-amazon-treats-its-pregnant-workforce-the-suit-seeks-reforms-of-amazon-s-policies-on-behalf-of-the-plaintiffs-and-a-class-of-similarly-situated-workers-as-well-as-compensation-for-past-and-ongoing-violations-of-the-law-nbsp">As the new lawsuit notes, the four plaintiffs are likely indicative of a bigger problem with how Amazon treats its pregnant workforce. The suit seeks reforms of Amazon’s policies on behalf of the plaintiffs and a class of “similarly situated workers,” as well as compensation for past and ongoing violations of the law.&nbsp;</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The number of affected workers will likely go up as word spreads about the possible class action lawsuit, and the filing notes that A Better Place fielded calls from “dozens” of pregnant Amazon employees in 2025 who said the company had denied them basic accommodations. So far this year, nearly 200 Amazon workers have called the organization’s free helpline, its president Inimai Chettiar told <em><a href="https://www.usatoday.com/story/life/health-wellness/2026/09/08/amazon-sued-pregnant-worker-rights-exclusive/91590740007/" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">USA Today</a></em>. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">While Amazon may be the focus of this lawsuit, it is not an isolated incident and speaks to what’s at stake if critical protections for pregnant workers are rolled back, according to Chettiar.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">&#8220;It actually sits inside a larger pattern of policy and rhetoric aimed at narrowing women&#8217;s roles and pushing them out of the workforce and back into a domestic role,&#8221; she told <em>USA Today</em>.</p>
