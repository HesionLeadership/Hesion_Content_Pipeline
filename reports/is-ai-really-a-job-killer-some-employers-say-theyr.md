# Is AI really a job killer? Some employers say they’re actually hiring more

**Source:** Fast Company  
**Published:** 2026-07-29T20:30:00  
**URL:** [https://www.fastcompany.com/91581912/is-ai-really-a-job-killer-some-employers-say-theyre-actually-hiring-more](https://www.fastcompany.com/91581912/is-ai-really-a-job-killer-some-employers-say-theyre-actually-hiring-more)

## Summary
While some companies like Visa are cutting jobs citing AI, a ZipRecruiter survey of 1,000+ hiring leaders finds 35% expect AI to increase headcount, with only 16% hiring at lower rates due to AI. Entry-level roles face the greatest displacement risk, and a notable gender divide exists in how leaders perceive AI's workforce impact.

## Organizational Psychology Angle
The story highlights how technological change reshapes job design and workforce composition rather than simply eliminating jobs—a pattern consistent with job demands-resources theory and research on technological unemployment. The finding that entry-level roles are most exposed while overall headcount may grow suggests organizations are restructuring role hierarchies, raising skill floors, and potentially eliminating the traditional developmental pipeline for early-career employees.

## Leadership Lesson
CHROs should resist binary thinking about AI as purely a job creator or destroyer and instead focus on workforce architecture: redesigning entry-level roles to preserve developmental pathways, investing in reskilling programs (as JPMorgan's Dimon models), and auditing whether AI-driven productivity gains are inadvertently closing the door on talent pipeline development. Additionally, leaders should be aware that gendered perceptions of AI's impact may reflect real differential exposure across job categories, warranting targeted communication and transition support.

## Original Story Summary
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The fear that <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/artificial-intelligence" title="AI">AI</a> is killing jobs has been fueled by ongoing layoffs across corporate America, most recently at <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91581050/visa-layoffs-today-jobs-slashed-ceo-memo-ai-acceleration" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">companies like Visa</a>. Just this week, the payments giant revealed plans to shed 7% of its head count, a move that will impact 2,600 employees. While <a href="https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-28/visa-to-cut-7-of-workforce-as-ceo-seeks-to-revamp-payments-firm" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">Bloomberg reported</a> that AI wasn’t the only factor driving the layoffs, CEO Ryan McInerney clearly cited it in his memo, noting AI would “shape the way work gets done at Visa.” Some of its peers, <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91538995/tech-layoffs-due-to-ai-this-week-cloudflare-paypal-coinbase-upwork" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">like PayPal</a>, have also made bold cuts to their workforce in recent months. Companies in various sectors have been <a href="https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-28/ai-wipes-out-customer-service-jobs-at-microsoft-uber-cba" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">slashing customer service jobs</a>, replacing some of them entirely with artificial intelligence. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">But as researchers have repeatedly pointed out, these layoffs still don’t point to widespread job displacement. In fact, a <a href="https://www.ziprecruiter-research.org/economic-insights-research/ai-employer-report-2026" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">new report from ZipRecruiter</a> suggests that some companies may actually be adding jobs due to AI. When ZipRecruiter surveyed over a thousand recruiters and <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/hiring" title="Hiring">hiring</a> leaders, more than a third of them—nearly 35%—said AI will increase head count at their company in the near future. Almost a quarter of them said hiring due to AI had already increased accordingly; over a third said AI had already enabled them to accelerate their recruiting process. Nearly as many respondents (32.5%) said the mix of jobs would change in the next three to five years as a result of AI, even if head count didn’t dramatically change. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">By comparison, the number of people who said they were actively hiring at a lower rate due to AI was far smaller, at 16%, and only 14.4% said they had plans to decrease head count in the coming years. What does seem to be changing, however, is job expectations: Nearly three-quarters of people surveyed said AI skills were now either a major advantage or requirement for certain roles; over half of employers also expect that their workers will now be more <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/productivity" title="Productivity">productive</a>. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Economists and researchers <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91435784/how-ai-became-the-scapegoat-for-the-current-wave-of-mass-layoffs" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">have warned</a> for some time now that the narrative around AI-related job displacement is misleading and overestimates the impact of AI thus far. Even AI leaders like OpenAI&#8217;s Sam Altman are now <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91548418/sam-altman-is-delighted-to-be-wrong-about-ai-destroying-jobs" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">backing away from their assertions</a> that the technology would eliminate jobs en masse. At companies like JPMorgan Chase, AI-related productivity gains have led to <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91573524/jamie-dimon-says-jpmorgan-has-slashed-40-of-jobs-in-some-departments-thanks-to-ai" id="https://www.fastcompany.com/91573524/jamie-dimon-says-jpmorgan-has-slashed-40-of-jobs-in-some-departments-thanks-to-ai" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank" type="link">significant job changes</a> in some divisions but have not meaningfully reduced overall head count or expenses. “We are preparing to make sure we can retrain our people,” CEO Jamie Dimon said recently. “We have had discrete areas where we did reduce jobs by 30% or 40%, and most of those people [were] offered jobs elsewhere.”</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">There is, however, evidence that entry-level jobs are at risk: ZipRecruiter found that those jobs were particularly exposed and that nearly 40% of employers had started reassigning entry-level tasks—like data entry and processing—to AI. For almost a third of companies, AI has raised the bar for entry-level candidates, a shift that echoes the experience of <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91552404/ai-isnt-the-real-reason-college-grads-cant-find-jobs" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">some young workers</a> navigating the job market. </p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">ZipRecruiter’s findings also captured another factor that might influence how employers talk about the effects of AI on their workforce. According to the survey, male respondents were more likely than female respondents to say that AI would help create jobs at their company. This gender divide in how leaders think AI will impact jobs also reflects a broader split in sentiment between men and women. On the whole, women tend to be <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91577494/men-are-more-likely-to-consult-ai-for-a-pay-bump" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">more skeptical of AI</a> or worry about <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91575822/ai-may-finally-make-womens-work-valuable-but-will-women-benefit" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">how it might impact them</a>—and given they dominate <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/91547256/ai-is-already-killing-the-executive-assistant-job" rel="noreferrer noopener" target="_blank">some of the sectors</a> that are most exposed to AI, it’s not hard to imagine why.</p>
