# Steve Jobs said great leaders make 1 difficult choice most managers avoid

**Source:** Fast Company  
**Published:** 2026-09-03T08:30:00  
**URL:** [https://www.fastcompany.com/91596741/steve-jobs-great-leaders-1-difficult-choice-most-managers-avoid](https://www.fastcompany.com/91596741/steve-jobs-great-leaders-1-difficult-choice-most-managers-avoid)

## Summary
The article argues that great leaders, as exemplified by Steve Jobs's philosophy that 'my job is not to be easy on people, my job is to make them better,' must choose honest, growth-oriented feedback over comfort-preserving avoidance. It outlines a framework: before challenging employees, leaders should ensure they've provided clear expectations, resources, authority, and removed obstacles, then deliver timely, specific, actionable feedback.

## Organizational Psychology Angle
The article addresses the tension between psychological safety and developmental challenge — a well-studied dynamic in organizational psychology where effective leadership requires both supportive environments and honest performance feedback to drive growth.

## Leadership Lesson
CHROs and COOs should build cultures where difficult feedback conversations are normalized rather than avoided, but only after ensuring leaders have fulfilled their own obligations — clarifying expectations, providing resources, and removing barriers. The practical takeaway: pair every challenging conversation with a genuine offer of support and obstacle removal.

## Original Story Summary
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">There is a dangerous idea floating around the&nbsp;<a href="https://www.inc.com/marcel-schwantes/signs-indicators-workplace-culture-wrong-direction-leadership/91356513">workplace</a>: If you care about employees, you should avoid making them uncomfortable.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That sounds compassionate. Sometimes it is. Other times, it is managerial cowardice disguised as&nbsp;<a href="https://www.inc.com/marcel-schwantes/its-official-how-to-be-a-servant-leader-comes-down-to-6-behaviors-says-research/90984161">servant leadership</a>.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Leaders who avoid difficult conversations leave people guessing. And capable employees miss opportunities to improve because nobody respected them enough to tell them the truth.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Steve Jobs addressed this tension in a 2008 <em>Fortune</em> interview. Describing his responsibility as Apple’s CEO, he said:</p>



<blockquote class="wp-block-quote is-layout-flow wp-block-quote-is-layout-flow">
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">My job is not to be easy on people. My job is to make them better.</p>
</blockquote>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That line lands differently today. Employees are exhausted. Teams are navigating constant change,&nbsp;<a href="https://www.inc.com/artificial-intelligence">artificial intelligence</a>&nbsp;is rewriting jobs, and many managers are leading people they rarely see in person.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Yet the essential leadership decision remains the same: Will you prioritize comfort over hard conversations, or will you help push people to grow and become better?</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-challenge-people-and-clear-the-path">Challenge people and clear the path</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Jobs’s full explanation deserves more attention than the famous quote. He described his job as bringing different parts of Apple together, clearing obstacles, securing resources for critical projects, and pushing talented people to improve.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That changes the equation considerably. A leader cannot demand exceptional work while withholding the information, authority, time, or tools required to produce it.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Before questioning an employee’s performance, a leader should ask:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>Have I made the expected result clear?</li>



<li>Have I explained why it matters?</li>



<li>Does this person have the authority and resources to deliver?</li>



<li>Did I provide useful feedback before the problem became serious?</li>



<li>What obstacle have I failed to remove?</li>
</ul>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-give-feedback-people-can-actually-use">Give feedback people can actually use</h2>

When having crucial conversations with employees, useful feedback is required, but it must be timely and specific. <p class="wp-block-paragraph">This matters even more when work is changing quickly. Employees cannot develop new judgment, adapt to unfamiliar technology, or take intelligent risks if every mistake becomes evidence that they do not belong.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-the-real-test-of-leadership">The real test of leadership</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Making people better is not something leaders do&nbsp;<em>to</em>&nbsp;employees. It is something they pursue&nbsp;<em>with</em>&nbsp;them.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That requires leaders to see potential, name the gap honestly, provide support, and remain accountable for the environment they create. It also means recognizing when someone cannot or will not meet the standard. Human-centered leadership does not require carrying persistent underperformance forever. It requires handling it clearly and fairly.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Jobs’s quote still holds up, provided leaders understand the responsibility hidden inside it. Your job is not to keep everyone comfortable. Your job is to create the conditions in which capable people can do meaningful work, hear the truth, and become more than they were when they arrived.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">My advice? Think of one person on your team who is capable of more. Have the conversation you have been postponing, but enter it with this question: “What support or obstacle removal do you need from me to reach the standard we both know you can meet?”</p>
