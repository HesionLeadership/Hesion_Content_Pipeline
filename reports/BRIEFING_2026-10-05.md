## This Week's Themes
The dominant theme this week is the gap between stated organizational policy and lived reality: **Pentagon stops using Anthropic AI tools months after blacklisting company, BBC told** (BBC Business) and **Amazon responds to data center backlash, says it no longer uses NDAs** (TechCrunch) both show institutions announcing policy shifts that lag far behind—or get undercut by—actual operational practice and eroded trust. A second theme is AI safety culture under strain, with **OpenAI's safety firings raise awkward questions** (Fortune) and **OpenAI safety employee resigns, claiming the company's 'culture is broken'** (TechCrunch) both surfacing questions about whether fast-moving AI labs have the redundancy and internal trust mechanisms of high-reliability industries. A third theme is leadership structure and succession risk, seen across **David Ellison just brought in a co-CEO to run his new empire: Meet Ynon Kreiz** (CNBC Business), **David Ellison and Ynon Kreiz announce Skydance leadership team** (CNBC Business), **Goldman Sachs CEO succession planning faces one big problem** (CNBC Business), and **Mattel names Roger Lynch as CEO, replacing Ynon Kreiz** (CNBC Business). Finally, a quieter but persistent thread on overwork and autonomy runs through **The rise of the 'two-job job': 61% of workers say they're now doing more than one role** (Fortune) and **Corgi Says Its 7-Day Workweek Is Voluntary. Experts Say That Answers the Wrong Question** (Inc), echoing the trust-vs-control tension in **'We need to trust our employees': This billionaire CEO went from counting cars in the parking lot past 6pm to embracing hybrid work** (Fortune).

## Top Story
**'We need to trust our employees': This billionaire CEO went from counting cars in the parking lot past 6pm to embracing hybrid work** (Fortune). This matters because it's a rare, candid first-person admission from a CEO that surveillance-based management (literally counting cars) was replaced by trust-based, output-driven leadership—a direct, usable case study for any CHRO auditing RTO policy against actual business goals rather than optics.

## Best Newsletter Candidate
**OpenAI safety employee resigns, claiming the company's 'culture is broken'** (TechCrunch) has the deepest org psych angle of the batch. Robinson's resignation letter is a direct, explicit application of High-Reliability Organization theory—he names the specific mechanism (nuclear plants and airports build in redundancy and deliberate pacing; OpenAI's "iterative deployment" trades that for speed) and even flags a structural hiring failure (no colleagues with cross-industry safety expertise). This gives Pete a 500+ word newsletter with a clean framework: define HRO theory, show how Robinson's critique maps onto it, then pivot to a diagnostic CHROs/COOs can run in their own high-stakes functions—are speed incentives structurally at odds with your safety culture, and have you deliberately imported expertise from industries that have already solved this problem? It also connects naturally to the OpenAI firings story as a companion data point, letting Pete build a two-pronged narrative about psychological safety and internal escalation failure inside one of the most-watched companies in tech.

## Best Workshop/Keynote Example
**David Ellison just brought in a co-CEO to run his new empire: Meet Ynon Kreiz** (CNBC Business) is a clean, real-world illustration of shared/co-leadership structure design—visionary founder paired with operational integrator. It's a great live exercise: have the room debate where decision rights should sit when "strategy vs. operations" roles are split, and whether a leader's prior skill set (cost-cutting turnarounds) transfers to a different challenge (innovation-driven integration).
