# Morning Briefing — August 15, 2026

## This Week's Themes
**Psychological safety is under siege from multiple directions.** A striking number of today's stories converge on the same fundamental problem: leaders are actively — sometimes deliberately — destroying the conditions people need to do their best work. Whether it's anger as a management tool (Inc), a CTO publicly humiliating an employee's question (Meta), or perfectionism cultures crushing experimentation (Inc, MIT Sloan), the throughline is that leaders are saying they want innovation and engagement while behaving in ways that guarantee neither. **Innovation and incentive misalignment** is the second dominant theme — three separate stories (Inc on incentives, Inc on culture flaws, MIT Sloan on frontline innovation) all argue that organizations are structurally designed to kill the very innovation they claim to want, and that the fix is systemic, not motivational. **AI's messy collision with human judgment** is emerging as a critical operational challenge: from biased recruitment algorithms disadvantaging women (BBC) to employees covertly reshaping AI outputs to protect their credibility (HBR), organizations are deploying AI faster than they're redesigning the accountability and fairness structures around it. Finally, **cultural inertia and leadership team dysfunction** round out the picture — IBM's CEO admitting culture moved too slowly and the "invisible tax" of co-founder friction both point to the same lesson: the biggest threats to organizational performance are often the ones leadership is slowest to name.

## Top Story
**"When Employees Are Held Accountable for AI-Generated Decisions" (HBR)** — This is the story Pete's audience needs to sit with. As AI tools proliferate across every function, CHROs and COOs are building a ticking time bomb: employees who must own decisions they didn't actually make, and who respond by quietly editing, masking, or overriding AI outputs to protect themselves. This isn't a technology problem — it's an accountability design problem, and it's the kind of organizational systems issue that Hesion is built to address. The practical implications are immediate: if your clients are deploying AI without redesigning who owns what, they're getting neither the AI's value nor their people's honest judgment.

## Best Newsletter Candidate
**"Your Managers Aren't Blocking Innovation. Your Incentives Are" (Inc)** — This deserves the deep-dive treatment because it reframes a problem Pete's clients constantly bring to him ("our managers resist change") as a systems design failure, not a people failure. There's a rich org psych vein to mine here: reinforcement theory, principal-agent dynamics, the gap between espoused values and enacted reward systems, and the specific ways performance management and promotion criteria encode risk aversion. Pete could walk readers through a practical incentive audit framework — what to look for, what questions to ask, and how to redesign manager-level incentives so they actually reward the behaviors leadership says it wants. This is a 500-word piece that practically writes itself and positions Hesion squarely in the "we fix the system, not just the people" lane.

## Best Workshop/Keynote Example
**"'I've had to Botox my CV': Are AI Recruitment Tools Affecting Women's Careers?" (BBC)** — This is a vivid, memorable illustration of how well-intentioned technology can encode and amplify structural bias. The "Botox my CV" metaphor alone is sticky enough to carry a keynote segment. It perfectly illustrates the concept of **adverse impact in selection systems** — and the broader principle that automation doesn't eliminate human bias, it scales it. In a workshop setting, Pete could use this to walk leaders through the difference between efficiency and fairness in talent acquisition, and why CHROs must audit algorithmic tools with the same rigor they'd apply to any selection instrument.
