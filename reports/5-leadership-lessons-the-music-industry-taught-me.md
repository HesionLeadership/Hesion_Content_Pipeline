## Original Story Summary
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Spend enough time around live music and you quickly learn that the talent standing in the spotlight is only a small part of what makes a great show. Behind every successful performance is an entire team of talented people working together to make awesome moments happen.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I’ve spent much of my career working in the music industry, from <a href="https://www.fastcompany.com/section/marketing" title="Marketing">marketing</a> and business development to managing independent venues and festivals, with stops along the way at Graceland, Fun Fun Fun Fest, DoStuff Media, and SXSW. I’ve also spent plenty of time playing music myself, including a period when I was pretty convinced I’d be the next Dave Matthews.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Today, as Huntsville, Alabama’s Music Officer, I’ve found that many lessons I learned working with artists, agents, promoters, production teams, business executives, interns, and others have been just as valuable in government as in the music business.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Here are five leadership lessons the music industry taught me that apply to most professions.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-1-if-you-say-you-re-going-to-do-something-do-it"><strong>1. IF YOU SAY YOU’RE GOING TO DO SOMETHING, DO IT.</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Reliability is a highly underrated leadership quality. One of the quickest ways to lose your team’s confidence is surprisingly simple: Tell someone you’re going to do something and then don’t follow through.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The lead guitarist might have incredible talent, but if they’re consistently late and unprepared, the band probably won’t be enthusiastic about working with them.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Whether it’s finishing a project, making an introduction, or getting someone the information they need to move forward, people rely on those commitments. Not delivering on responsibilities can throw everything off course. Trust is ultimately built through what’s delivered.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-2-you-don-t-need-to-be-the-smartest-person-in-the-room"><strong>2. YOU DON’T NEED TO BE THE SMARTEST PERSON IN THE ROOM.</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Nobody has all the answers, and being in a leadership position doesn’t automatically make your ideas the best ones. Some of the most creative and effective solutions I’ve encountered came from people closest to the work.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Think about everything involved in producing a concert. The sound engineer understands things about audio the promoter probably doesn’t. The lighting director has perfected their craft. The artist, stage manager, marketing team, and venue operators have their own areas of expertise. How much harder would that operation be if everyone had to defer to one person who believed they knew more than everybody else?</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Create an environment where people feel valued and empowered to speak up, challenge ideas, and suggest better ways of doing things.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-3-sometimes-the-best-leaders-get-out-of-the-way"><strong>3. SOMETIMES THE BEST LEADERS GET OUT OF THE WAY.</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Musicians, technicians, stagehands, managers, security staff, and ticket sellers have specific responsibilities. When everyone knows what they’re doing, the operation runs so smoothly that most in the audience don’t think about the work happening behind the scenes.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Sometimes a well-intentioned person jumping in to “help” can make things harder. The same applies to leadership. If you’ve assembled a great team, trust people to do their jobs. Your team should know you’re there when they need resources or guidance, but there’s a fine line between supporting and micromanaging.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-4-be-a-chameleon-without-being-fake"><strong>4. BE A CHAMELEON WITHOUT BEING FAKE.</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In music, I worked with artists, business executives, concert promoters, venue operators, and community organizations. In government, I’ve added elected officials, city departments, and other stakeholders to that mix.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Each group has its own priorities and communication styles. Learn how to adapt to those environments without compromising who you are. Understand the room you’re walking into. What matters to and motivates the people you’re talking with? What do they need to understand to make a decision?</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">There’s a distinction between adapting your communication and claiming expertise you don’t have. Don’t change your values based on who’s in the room and don’t underestimate how quickly talented, experienced people can recognize when someone isn’t being genuine.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-5-earn-the-right-to-take-bigger-swings"><strong>5. EARN THE RIGHT TO TAKE BIGGER SWINGS.</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Making the transition into local government from a music industry career came with culture shock. The music business naturally rewards creativity, experimentation, and taking risks. Government, for good reason, places greater emphasis on process, accountability, and responsibly allocating public resources.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Those two approaches don’t have to be mutually exclusive. Some of the most meaningful progress happens when you bring an entrepreneurial mindset into an environment not accustomed to doing things differently. The key is understanding that you must earn the trust to experiment.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Plenty of people seem more interested in being viewed as innovative rather than doing the work necessary to make their ideas successful. Making a bold move to prove you’re a bold leader isn’t a good strategy. Instead, focus on building credibility through consistent execution, responsible decisions, and a track record of delivering results.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Trust is organizational capital. When you follow through on a commitment, solve a problem, or demonstrate sound judgment, you build more of it. Eventually, that credibility allows you to propose bigger ideas and challenge conventional thinking. These calculated risks might otherwise struggle to gain support.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">When you do take big swings, be thoughtful about potential consequences. Make sure the people involved understand the vision, the risks, and what you’re trying to accomplish. The goal should be to build something meaningful that creates opportunities and continues delivering value years down the road.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading" id="h-leaders-it-s-not-all-about-you"><strong>LEADERS: IT’S NOT ALL ABOUT YOU</strong></h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The most important things I’ve learned about leadership came from working alongside talented people who knew how to get things done under pressure, and without much recognition.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The best live performances are successful because people understand their roles, trust one another, communicate effectively, and take pride in contributing to something bigger than themselves. The same is true of great organizations.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">While the headliner gets applause, the entire team makes the show possible. Great leaders make sure everyone gets a standing ovation.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><em>Matt Mandrella is music officer of the City of Huntsville.</em></p>
